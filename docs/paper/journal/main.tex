\title[Reader-Faithfulness in LLM-Based Narrative Assistants]{Reader-Faithfulness in LLM-Based Narrative Assistants: Measuring Parametric Memory Leakage and Future-Information Leakage in Processing Pipelines}

\author[1]{\fnm{Adhyan} \sur{Jain}}\email{adhyan.jain2024@vitstudent.ac.in}

\author[1]{\fnm{Atharva Sheersh} \sur{Pandey}}\email{atharva.sheersh2024@vitstudent.ac.in}

\author[1]{\fnm{Aryan} \sur{Gahlot}}\email{aryan.gahlot2024@vitstudent.ac.in}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Shashank Mouli} \sur{Satapathy}}\email{shashankmouli.s@vit.ac.in}

\affil[1]{\orgdiv{School of Computer Science and Engineering}, \orgname{Vellore Institute of Technology}, \orgaddress{\city{Vellore}, \postcode{632014}, \state{Tamil Nadu}, \country{India}}}

\abstract{\textbf{Problem and gap.} An assistant that summarises a novel or answers questions about it should not tell the reader what the book has not yet revealed. Existing studies ask whether models remember books or keep a timeline consistent. They do not ask whether a hidden identity leaks before its reveal, or whether a processing pipeline lets later chapters shape what is shown earlier.
\textbf{Objective.} We define reader-faithfulness as non-interference from the unread text and measure two leakage channels: parametric memory, and future information introduced by the pipeline.
\textbf{Methods and data.} A reveal-time task on public-domain novels shows passages from before an explicit reveal sentence under original names, letter ciphers and cue-preserving pseudonyms (38 exploratory and 68 pre-registered confirmatory events). We also rebuild a narrative knowledge graph and several generic assistant designs from the reader's prefix and compare them with whole-volume builds, using counterfactual suffixes and planted suffix canaries.
\textbf{Principal results.} A commercial reference model named hidden identities early more often under original names on the exploratory books (+26.0 points). The confirmatory study, on mostly little-known books, found no gap (+0.1 points, 95\% CI \textminus5.0 to +5.2). A persona-derivation stage diverged from a prefix-only build in 41 of 685 character-cuts, and model annotators judged 1.7--3.5\% of cuts to be genuine premature disclosures. Whole-volume character cards named the hidden identity in 18.5\% (judge rule) and 24.6\% (text rule) of events. A reader-position marker did not stop long-context answers from quoting later text (40.6\% of events), and alias-merged retrieval leaked in 19.4\% of events against 1.4\% for a prefix-built index. Building from the prefix removed the observed leakage, at a measured cost.
\textbf{Limitations.} Events are model-selected (two human annotators confirmed the reveal as explicit in 34 of 36 checked events), the oracle covers only explicit reveal sentences, most other labels come from models, and the memory effect depends on familiarity and on the models tested.}

\keywords{Reader-faithfulness, Spoilers, Parametric memory, Information leakage, Narrative understanding, Large language models, Evaluation methodology}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Imagine a reader halfway through a mystery who asks an assistant who ``the masked guest'' really is. The book answers that question in its last chapters, and the assistant should not answer it sooner. Language models are now routinely asked to recap long novels, answer questions about them and keep character guides for serial fiction. Every one of these uses carries a reader position: at chapter $c$, the system may draw only on what the first $c$ chapters say. We call a system \emph{reader-faithful} when its output at position $c$ does not depend on the rest of the book. The property is simple to state. As this paper shows, it is also easy to break without anyone noticing.

An assistant can break it in two ways. The first is \emph{parametric memory}. A model trained on text that includes the book may already know who the masked guest is, so cutting its input down to the reader's prefix does not stop it from saying so. The second is \emph{future information introduced by the pipeline}. A preprocessing stage, a character card, an entity index or a long-context prompt may have been built from the whole volume, in which case later chapters shape what is served at an earlier position even when every individual model call sees only the prefix. Memorisation of books has been studied directly \cite{chang2023speak,fu2026summarization}, and renaming is a standard way to control for it \cite{jiang2024beyond,deusser2026anonymization}. The pipeline channel resembles look-ahead bias in financial forecasting \cite{glasserman2023lookahead} and the problem of point-in-time correctness in stream processing \cite{akidau2015dataflow}. Within the search described in Section~\ref{sec:rw-spoiler}, we found no study that measures either channel as disclosure at a reader position.

The question came up while we were building a reading assistant and a narrative knowledge graph for long web serials, where early disclosure was a practical obstacle. Here we treat it purely as a measurement problem.

Three things are missing from earlier work. Memorisation studies score recall, through name cloze, source identification or summary quality, and do not check whether an answer given mid-book states something the book has not yet stated. Renaming studies show that performance falls when names change, but cannot tell memory tied to a specific name apart from the reading cues that a name carries, such as gender. Temporal and spoiler-aware narrative systems keep chapter-safe state, yet none of them tests whether a representation derived at one position changes when only the unread text changes. These gaps lead to three research questions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} Under what conditions does a model reveal a character's identity before an explicit narrative reveal, and how does this depend on original names, renaming, pseudonyms and book familiarity?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} How much future information enters reader-positioned character state through derived facts, whole-volume cards and long-context inputs, and which repairs reduce this leakage?
\item \textbf{RQ3.} Can future-informed entity resolution or retrieval decisions disclose a hidden identity even when the passages served to the reader are restricted to the prefix?
\end{itemize}
Each research question has one matching contribution. For RQ1 we give a \emph{reveal-time evaluation protocol}. The book's own explicit reveal sentence, found by exact search, fixes the moment an identity becomes known; renaming arms with a cue-preserving control separate name-keyed memory from lost reading cues; and the analysis uses event-level inference across an exploratory phase and a pre-registered confirmatory phase. For RQ2 and RQ3 we give an \emph{audit of future-dependent narrative pipelines}, combining truncate-and-rebuild comparison, a static exposure screen, a counterfactual-suffix test and string-match suffix canaries. We apply it to a narrative knowledge graph and to generic assistant designs, and we keep pipeline dependence separate from genuine disclosure. Finally, we give a \emph{comparative assessment of mitigations with measured trade-offs}: evidence-horizon versioning, prefix-built character cards, physical context truncation, prefix-causal indexing and pseudonymisation, each set against the naive repair it replaces and reported with the values it withholds and the time it costs.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work and Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the problem formally. Sections~\ref{sec:method} and \ref{sec:setup} describe the methods and the experimental setup, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}, \ref{sec:limits} and \ref{sec:conclusion} discuss them, list the limitations and conclude.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{LLM Memorisation and Book Familiarity}\label{sec:rw-mem}

Chang et al.\ \cite{chang2023speak} used a name-cloze membership query to estimate which books ChatGPT and GPT-4 had memorised. Memorisation tracked how often a book's passages appear on the web, and the models did better on downstream tasks for books they had memorised. Fu et al.\ \cite{fu2026summarization} compared summaries a model writes from memory with summaries it writes from the full text; the full text usually, though not always, produced the better summary. Studies of benchmark contamination reach similar conclusions about evaluation data \cite{sainz2023contamination,golchin2024timetravel}, and extraction studies measure verbatim memorisation \cite{carlini2019secret,carlini2023quantifying}. Together these show that models partly know familiar books, and they measure that knowledge through recall, cloze accuracy or summary quality. We measure something else: whether an answer given at a reader position names an identity the text has not yet revealed, when the only justified answer is that the identity is still undetermined. A model could do well on name cloze and still keep a secret, or the other way round, so our outcome does not follow from theirs. We carry the familiarity question over to premature disclosure; we do not set out to establish book memorisation itself.

\subsection{Renaming and Memorisation-Constrained Evaluation}\label{sec:rw-rename}

Renaming characters is a well-established way to limit memorisation in evaluation. Jiang and Ferraro \cite{jiang2024beyond} replaced character names while keeping implicit cues and saw accuracy on popular fiction fall from 96\% to 72\%, with drops of up to 18\% on character-understanding tasks. Gu et al.\ \cite{gu2026synthworlds} built parallel worlds, one mapped to real entities and one synthetic, and measured how much real-world knowledge helps. Deußer et al.\ \cite{deusser2026anonymization} studied anonymisation as a privacy--utility trade-off over eleven benchmarks and found that reversible methods which keep entities distinct cost less than redaction. We use renaming in this standard sense, but on a reveal-time outcome, and we add a cue-preserving pseudonym arm. The reason is that a letter cipher removes the memorised name and also cues such as gender and name type. If pseudonyms bring ordinary reading accuracy back while the advantage of original names remains, the most consistent explanation is memory keyed to the specific name. Other explanations are possible, because any transformation can change other properties of a text, so we present this as an inference and not as a demonstrated mechanism.

\subsection{Reader-Position-Aware Narrative State and Coreference}\label{sec:rw-state}

Several systems track narrative state over time. TimeChara \cite{ahn2024timechara} evaluates point-in-time hallucination in role-playing models and lists spoiler avoidance among its motivations, but its errors are contradictions of a character's timeline, not disclosures tied to a reveal. Ma et al.\ \cite{ma2026script} benchmark whether interactive narrators built from movie synopses keep their commitments over long interactions. Writer-memory systems store chapter-safe or belief-scoped story state. The Narrative World Model \cite{saifullah2026nwm} reads every system through chapter-safe evidence and separates the order of events from the narration that reveals them, and NarraWorld \cite{tian2026narraworld} keeps a belief view for each character. E$^2$RAG \cite{zhang2025e2rag} holds entities and events in separate subgraphs to keep retrieval over novels temporally and causally consistent. These are the closest existing designs. Our question complements theirs: we ask whether a stage computed with access to later chapters changes what is served at an earlier position, which none of them tests.

The entity layer of such systems usually comes from literary coreference resources (Table~\ref{tab:rw}). Their labels are omniscient: a mention is linked to whoever it refers to in the finished book. KoCoNovel \cite{kim2024koconovel} is the exception, since it also releases a version annotated from the reader's perspective. Omniscient resolution is not the same as reader-position resolution. An alias correctly merged with its true identity is, before the reveal, exactly the merge a reader-faithful system must keep hidden. Resolution quality and reader-faithfulness are therefore different properties, and the audit in Section~\ref{sec:m-pipeline} measures the second one.

\subsection{Temporal Retrieval and Spoiler-Safe Assistants}\label{sec:rw-spoiler}

Research on spoilers has mostly been about detecting them in reviews \cite{wan2019spoiler}, not about stopping an assistant from producing them. Assistants usually ground their answers in a book through retrieval-augmented generation \cite{lewis2020rag}, and long-context models can now read a whole volume at once, although how much they use a piece of information depends on where it sits in the input \cite{liu2024lost}. Some products already try to be spoiler-safe. One database vendor documents a pattern that stamps each chapter with a synthetic time and filters recall by the reader's position, and notes that passages are not fully gated by that filter \cite{surrealdb2026spoiler}. A reading companion is described as giving the model only the chapters the reader has finished \cite{aiindigo2026paige}. These are vendor descriptions, not evaluations, and we cite them only to show that position filtering and input truncation exist as designs. Two ideas in our method are borrowed. Attaching to each value the latest input time it depends on comes from watermarking in stream processing \cite{akidau2015dataflow}, and planting a unique token and searching outputs for it comes from memorisation testing \cite{carlini2019secret}. The new part is applying them to reader positions and the measurements in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Our literature and product searches, logged in the project repository between 15 and 23 October 2026, turned up no study that measures selection leakage, uses suffix canaries at a reader position, or audits a narrative graph by truncation and rebuilding. That statement covers our search only; it is not a claim that no such work exists.

\section{Problem Formulation and Reader-Faithfulness}\label{sec:problem}

Let a book $B=(b_1,\dots,b_n)$ be a sequence of text units, either chapters or character offsets. The reader is at position $c$, the prefix is $P(c)=(b_1,\dots,b_c)$, and the suffix is whatever remains unread. A narrative assistant is a function $F$ of the book and the position. Its output $F(B,c)$ covers the final response and every intermediate representation that response can draw on, including derived facts in a knowledge graph, character cards, entity-merge decisions and the set of retrieved passages.

\begin{definition}[Reader-faithfulness]\label{def:rf}
A component $X$ of $F$ is reader-faithful at position $c$ if $F_X(B,c)=F_X(B',c)$ for every book $B'$ whose prefix at $c$ equals $P(c)$.
\end{definition}

In words, changing the unread suffix must not change what is served at $c$; it is a non-interference criterion. Two practical tests follow. If $B'$ is the prefix on its own, we get the \emph{truncate-and-rebuild} test, $F(B,c)=F(P(c),c)$. If $B'$ is the book with a rewritten suffix, we get the \emph{counterfactual-suffix} test. Both are necessary consequences of Definition~\ref{def:rf}. Neither is sufficient, since each checks only one alternative suffix. Nor is the definition a test of correctness: a faithful system can still be wrong, and a difference between $F(B,c)$ and $F(P(c),c)$ need not reveal anything a reader could not already work out.

For this reason we evaluate two separate things. \emph{Behavioural leakage} is an actual disclosure of a hidden identity or future fact before the book states it. \emph{Pipeline dependence} is any change in the output, or in an intermediate representation, when only the unread suffix changes. Dependence can be measured without labels. Deciding whether a particular dependence amounts to a disclosure means judging what the prefix supports, which is why the audit in Section~\ref{sec:m-pipeline} reports both.

Parametric memory works differently. A model whose training data included $B$ can name a hidden identity from the prefix alone; $F(P(c),c)$ then leaks by itself, and truncation is no help. We measure this channel through behaviour. In an \emph{event} $e$, an alias (``the Opera ghost'') is later identified with a named character (``Erik'') by an explicit sentence of the book, which we call the \emph{reveal}. The \emph{cutoff} is a position before the reveal. Each \emph{pre-reveal item} shows a passage that ends before the cutoff, marks one mention of the alias, lists the event's cast plus the option \textsc{undetermined}, and asks who the marked expression refers to. The only justified answer is \textsc{undetermined}. Giving the true identity is \emph{premature naming}. Let $y_{ei}^{a}\in\{0,1\}$ record premature naming on item $i$ of event $e$ under arm $a$, where the arms are original names ($A$), the mean of two independent letter ciphers ($B$), and cue-preserving pseudonyms ($B3$). The \emph{leakage gap} is the difference in premature-naming proportions, in percentage points:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap}
\Delta_{AB}=p_A-p_B,\qquad
p_a^{\mathrm{item}}=\frac{\sum_e\sum_{i=1}^{n_e}y_{ei}^{a}}{\sum_e n_e},\qquad
p_a^{\mathrm{event}}=\frac{1}{E}\sum_{e=1}^{E}\frac{1}{n_e}\sum_{i=1}^{n_e}y_{ei}^{a}.
\end{equation}
The exploratory analyses use the item-pooled proportion $p^{\mathrm{item}}$, which gives more weight to events with more pre-reveal items. The pre-registered confirmatory analysis uses the event-weighted proportion $p^{\mathrm{event}}$, where each event counts once. In both cases, uncertainty comes from resampling events, never items (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The \emph{recovered fraction} $\rho=(p_{B3}-p_B)/(p_A-p_B)$ is the share of the gap that comes back when name cues are restored. A value near 1 means the gap was about lost cues; a value near 0 means it is tied to the particular names.

The pipeline audit needs four more terms, and we use each only in the sense given here. A \emph{character-cut} is a pair (character, cut position $k$) for which both the full build and the prefix build serve a value of some derived trait at reader position $k$. A \emph{divergent fact} is a character-cut where the two builds serve different values. A fact is \emph{exposed} at $k$ when it is stamped at or before $k$ but its \emph{evidence horizon}, the latest position of the text it was computed from, lies after $k$. A \emph{genuine disclosure} is a divergent fact whose full-build value the text up to $k$ does not support. A divergence whose value the prefix already supports is dependence without disclosure.

Pipeline leakage itself comes in two forms. In a \emph{content leak}, text from the suffix reaches the model's input. In a \emph{selection leak}, the model sees only prefix text, but the choice of which prefix text to serve, or which entities to merge, was made using the suffix. Figure~\ref{fig:channels} puts the three channels side by side, together with the evaluation that detects each one and the place where each mitigation acts.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

The study runs in two experimental tracks that share the ideas of a reader position and an evidence horizon (Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}). Track 1 measures parametric memory using reveal-time events from public-domain novels (Sections~\ref{sec:m-corpus} and \ref{sec:m-memory}). Track 2 audits a narrative knowledge graph and several generic assistant designs for dependence on future text (Sections~\ref{sec:m-pipeline} and \ref{sec:m-mitigation}). The generic-assistant experiments reuse the events from Track 1.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering

\caption{Workflow of the two research tracks. Track 1 (parametric memory, RQ1) builds reveal-time events from public-domain novels and compares premature naming across arms A (original names), B and B2 (two letter ciphers) and B3 (cue-preserving pseudonyms). Track 2 (pipeline and assistant leakage, RQ2 and RQ3) compares a full build $F(B)$ with a prefix build $F(P(c))$, labels divergences, applies repairs and re-audits. \subsection{Corpus and Reveal-Time Event Construction}\label{sec:m-corpus}

Every event comes from a Project Gutenberg plain-text novel \cite{gutenberg} first published before 1928. We remove the Project Gutenberg header and footer (everything before the \texttt{*** START} marker and after the \texttt{*** END} marker) and strip carriage returns, and make no other change to the text. Matching ignores whitespace differences: each sentence is turned into a regular expression in which any run of whitespace matches any other, so line wrapping in the plain text has no effect.

An event consists of a book, one or more alias surfaces, an identity name, a cast list of seven to nine names that includes the identity, a verbatim reveal sentence and a cutoff anchor. The reveal sentence has to occur exactly once in the book. If it occurs twice or not at all, the event is dropped rather than disambiguated. The cutoff is placed at the start of the nearest chapter heading before the reveal. Because tables of contents repeat the headings, we use the last match of the heading pattern that begins before the reveal. If the alias never appears before that heading, a fixed phrase at least 200 characters before the reveal serves as the cutoff instead. A deterministic verifier then checks five conditions: the cutoff comes before the reveal; the alias appears at least three times before the cutoff; no sentence before the cutoff contains both an alias surface and a capitalised token of the identity name; the identity and the aliases share no capitalised token, so the name itself gives nothing away; and the prefix mentions the cast often enough to build the plain controls of Section~\ref{sec:m-memory}.

Language models propose candidate events, and these rules then verify them. For the exploratory sets (PD v2, v3 and v6), a model drafter proposed events one book at a time. For the confirmatory set, a pre-registered pipeline searched sentences of books sampled by fame band for a fixed list of reveal cues (for example ``was none other than'', ``under the assumed name'' and ``revealed himself''). An open model then extracted the alias, the identity and the reveal sentence, and the reveal sentence had to appear verbatim in the passage. An auditor model from a different family labelled each reveal \textsc{explicit}, \textsc{implied} or \textsc{not supported} with respect to the claim that the alias is the identity, and the \textsc{explicit} events make up the confirmatory primary set. The exploratory events were audited afterwards by the commercial reference model, which marked 20 of the 31 v2 and v3 events \textsc{explicit}, 2 \textsc{implied} and 9 \textsc{not supported}.

What results is a \emph{reproducible, text-grounded oracle with model-assisted event selection}. Once an event has been proposed, matching and cutoff verification are deterministic, so anyone with the same texts and rules gets the same items and labels. Which events exist in the first place, and whether a given reveal sentence really identifies the alias, depend on model judgements. The oracle also records only what the book \emph{states}. An explicit reveal sentence is not necessarily the first point at which an attentive reader could guess the identity.

To check the oracle, three of the authors labelled a stratified, seeded subset of 36 events: 18 exploratory events spread across the three datasets, and 18 from the confirmatory primary set that include every high- and mid-fame event. Two annotators labelled each event independently, without seeing any model label, in an annotation tool that shows the reveal sentence in context alongside the same pre-reveal passages the models received. For each event they recorded whether they already knew the book's plot, whether the reveal sentence identifies the alias explicitly, only by implication or not at all, whether it reads as a revelation or as a reminder of something already said, whether a first-time reader of the earlier passages could name the identity (no, suspect or confident), and whether the event is valid. We summarise agreement with Fleiss' $\kappa$ \cite{fleiss1971measuring} and recompute the main gaps after removing every event that either annotator flagged. The annotators did not see model labels, but as authors they knew the study's hypotheses. The results appear in Section~\ref{sec:r-memory}.

\subsection{Parametric-Memory Intervention and Controls}\label{sec:m-memory}

Each pre-reveal item shows the last 1,200 characters of text up to the end of the sentence containing the sampled alias mention, with the mention wrapped in double brackets. We sample up to eight pre-reveal mentions per event (four in the confirmatory study), evenly spaced over the prefix, so items sit anywhere from a few hundred to several hundred thousand characters before the cutoff. The candidate list is the event's cast in a seeded random order plus \textsc{undetermined}, and the model replies with a single letter, held to the allowed set by a JSON schema where the runtime supports one. Two kinds of control item are built in the same way. \emph{Post-reveal items}, which are alias mentions after the reveal where the identity is the right answer, check that the task can be answered at all. \emph{Plain controls}, which are mentions of ordinary cast members before the cutoff, measure how well the model reads the passage under each arm.

The arms change nothing but names. Arm A shows the original text. Arm B replaces every proper-noun token, meaning any capitalised token of three or more letters that never appears in lowercase in the book, with a case- and length-preserving letter substitution drawn from a fixed per-book seed. Honorifics and other protected words stay as they are, substitutions that would collide with real words are redrawn, and the same mapping is applied to the window, the marked mention and the candidate list, so the correct letter is the same in every arm. Arm B2 repeats B with a second, independent seed. The analysis averages B and B2, so a gap that only one cipher produces does not count. Arm B3 keeps the cues a cipher destroys. It replaces the cast and alias name tokens with natural pseudonyms matched for gender (inferred from the pronouns that follow the name in the book) and for name type (given name or surname), and leaves every other proper noun under the arm-B cipher so that places and bystanders still offer no memory cue. Since answers are letters, mapping an answer back to an identity is simply a lookup in the event's candidate list. Window text and candidate order are the same across arms.

Two probes test memory directly. The closed-book probe asks who the alias is without giving any passage. The title-assisted probe gives only the book title and the alias. Familiarity in Section~\ref{sec:r-cue} is defined from the title-assisted probe as the number of five local models that name the identity. Before any gap is interpreted, each model must pass a validity gate on each dataset: the two ciphers must agree on at least 90\% of plain controls, and the original and cipher arms must agree on at least 80\% of items from synthetic stories with invented names, where memory cannot play a part. When pseudonyms restore control accuracy but the gap stays, we describe name-keyed memory as the most consistent explanation and do not claim to have shown the mechanism inside the model.

\subsection{Pipeline Audit and Counterfactual Suffix Tests}\label{sec:m-pipeline}

The system we audit is a narrative knowledge graph built for long web serials. It processes a volume as a whole and gives every fact three time axes: discourse order, story time, and \emph{knowledge time}, the position from which a reader could know the fact. Its reader query returns, for a character and a position $c$, the most recent value of each trait stamped at or before $c$. Because the whole volume is processed together, the moment a fact is written says nothing about when a reader could know it, so knowledge time has to be reconstructed from where the evidence is. The stage under audit is the \emph{persona stage}. It derives a character's gender, age band, speech register and archetype from the first twelve chapters in which the character appears, and stamps each trait at the chapter of first appearance. Take a character who first appears in chapter 1 but whose pronouns only show up in chapter 3. A query at chapter 2 is then served a gender that a build over chapters 1 and 2 could not contain.

\emph{Truncate-and-rebuild.} For each cut $k$, we run the persona stage twice: once on the full volume, and once on a copy of the database with every chapter, span, mention and segment after $k$ deleted. Both runs start from the same stored assignment of mentions to entities, which separates the persona stage from entity resolution. We then compare the values served at position $k+0.5$ for every character that has a persona fact in both builds. The audit covers three translated web serials, \emph{Lord of the Mysteries}, \emph{Omniscient Reader's Viewpoint} and \emph{Reverend Insanity}. Thirteen cuts yield 289 character-cuts and eleven further cuts add 396. Cuts are nested within each serial, so character-cuts are not independent.

\emph{Static exposure screen.} We read each trait's evidence horizon from the stage's code (gender, for instance, reads the first twelve attested chapters) and flag a fact as exposed at $k$ when its stamp is at most $k$ and its horizon is beyond $k$. The screen shows where divergence is possible without any rebuild. Since the dependency table was taken from the audited code itself, the screen is close to sound by construction, and we use it to certify cuts as safe, not to predict which ones diverge.

\emph{Disclosure labels.} A divergence alone does not mean a reader has been spoiled. For every divergent character-cut, two model annotators judged whether the text up to the cut supported the full-build value. Annotator 1 was the coding-assistant session that also implemented the repair; it wrote its labels without seeing any system value, and the labels were frozen by hash. Annotator 2 was the commercial reference model, run with a fixed prompt and a quote check that accepts an answer only if at least 80\% of its quoted words appear in order in the cited passage. A third pass with a more permissive prompt gives the other end of the range. No human labelled these cuts, because the serials are copyrighted and were never shared outside the project.

\emph{Counterfactual suffix.} We keep chapters up to $k$ byte-identical, swap the gendered pronouns in every later span, rebuild the whole volume and check whether the value served at $k$ moves. This measures dependence rather than divergence. It needs no truncation and no labels, and it works for closed-vocabulary outputs where a planted string could never appear.

Two further checks probe the mechanism and its generality. One is a synthetic volume of 40 chapters and 80 characters, with known genders and a known chapter in which each character's own pronoun cue first appears, generated in nine configurations of cue density with five seeds each. The other runs two English public-domain novels, \emph{Oliver Twist} and \emph{The Three Musketeers}, through the unchanged stage. We audit separately the stage's language-model path, which calls a local model for characters with at least 25 mentions.

\subsection{Leakage Mitigations and Generic-Assistant Experiments}\label{sec:m-mitigation}

Every mitigation follows one rule: nothing visible at position $c$ may be computed from evidence after $c$. They differ only in where the rule is enforced.

\emph{Evidence-horizon versioning} (EHV) recomputes the deterministic profile for each attested chapter $c$ from the evidence and cumulative mention counts of chapters up to $c$, and writes only the keys whose value changed, stamped at $c$. Since the reader query already returns the latest-stamped value per key, the value served at $k$ is exactly what a build over chapters up to $k$ would hold. Body bindings carry a matching \texttt{confirmed\_at} stamp. \emph{The anchored pronoun cue} deals with a side effect of EHV, namely that it withholds values whose pronouns settled early. It reads the first third-person pronoun after each mention, within the mention's own narration span, and is consulted only where the paragraph-level rule returns \emph{unknown}. Its setting was picked by leave-one-serial-out selection and scored on the held-out serial. \emph{The prefix-decided gate} replaces the whole-volume mention count that decided whether to call the language model for a character with a count over the chapters read so far. We compare these repairs with the obvious naive alternative, \emph{hiding every exposed fact}.

For generic assistants, we compare three designs with their reader-faithful counterparts on the confirmatory events, at a pre-registered cut just before the reveal chapter.

\emph{Whole-volume against versioned character cards.} A generator writes a card of at most 80 words about the aliased character from up to 12 passages spread over the alias's mentions. The whole-volume build W takes mentions from the entire book. The prefix build P takes only mentions before the cut and clips every passage at the cut. P serves as the truncate-and-rebuild reference and is identical to a card versioned by evidence horizon. Each build runs on original names and again under a frozen letter cipher. No model has seen the cipher names, so a leak in a renamed W card can only come from the input, and a renamed P card should never leak; this is our validity check. A card leaks if it tells the reader at the cut who the alias is, and we use two rules to decide that. The \emph{text rule} counts a leak when the card contains a name token of the identity that never occurs in the prefix. It is deterministic, but it only applies to the 19 events in which the identity's name first appears at the reveal. The \emph{judge rule} asks a fixed judge model, which is not one of the generators, whether the card states or clearly implies that the alias and the identity are the same person. It applies to all 54 events with alias mentions on both sides of the reveal. The two rules measure different things on different sets of events, and we never pool them. A robustness arm repeats W with an explicit instruction not to give away spoilers.

\emph{Suffix canaries and long context.} Here we alter only the suffix. Every identity name token after the cut becomes a freshly invented token, so the book still reveals the alias, but under a name no model has seen. We also plant one sentence, ``\emph{alias} had been born in \emph{invented place}'', after the first alias mention that follows the reveal. A hit is an exact string match of a planted token in the output, so it cannot be a false positive and cannot come from memory. We apply the canaries to the W card (with the P card as comparison) and to a long-context assistant that is told where the reader is. That assistant receives up to 24,000 characters before the cut, the line \texttt{[READER POSITION: the reader has read up to here]}, and up to 16,000 characters after it, including the reveal, with instructions to answer only from the text before the marker and to reveal nothing after it. The question is ``Who is \emph{alias}?''. The comparison condition is \emph{physical truncation}, meaning the same prompt with the suffix removed. The harness counts as valid only if the P-card and truncated conditions never produce a hit.

\emph{Alias-merged against prefix-built retrieval.} A canary cannot catch a leak that moves no suffix text, which motivates the selection test. It uses events whose identity is already named somewhere in the reader's prefix. At index time, a resolver model reads the reveal window and decides whether the alias and the identity are the same person. Retrieval for the alias then returns up to 12 passages spread over the pre-cut mentions of both names, each clipped at the cut (an assertion in the code enforces this), and the card prompt runs on cipher names so that memory cannot help. The comparison is a \emph{prefix-causal index} built from the prefix alone, which has no reveal to merge on. Leaks here are labelled by the judge model, and the judge was validated against the text rule only on events where that rule applies. The selection test is therefore where the judge is least validated, because both names appear in the input, which is exactly the situation in which a judge is most needed.

\emph{Pseudonymise and map back} is the mitigation for memory. It replaces the names in the input with pseudonyms, runs the model, and maps names in the answer back to the originals. It was pre-registered with a non-inferiority margin of 5 points on accuracy after the reveal and on plain controls.

\emph{Statistical protocol.} Items in the same event share a book, a reveal and often a passage, so we resample events, not items, throughout. Intervals for gaps are 95\% percentile intervals from an event-cluster bootstrap \cite{efron1993bootstrap,dror2018hitchhiker}, using 5,000 resamples (seed 20261019) for the exploratory analyses and 10,000 (seed 20261101) for the confirmatory one. When the exploratory analysis pools several models, their items are combined within each event before events are resampled, which keeps the dependence between items from the same model and between models answering the same event. Exploratory results come with two-sided exact sign tests over events. The confirmatory hypotheses were one-sided at $\alpha=0.05$, with a Holm correction \cite{holm1979simple} over the family \{H1, H2, H3, H5\} at the pre-registered family-wise level. The pooled confirmatory gap (H1) combines the per-model event-weighted estimates in a DerSimonian--Laird random-effects model \cite{dersimonian1986meta} with a Hartung--Knapp interval \cite{hartung2001tests}. We chose random effects because the three models stand in for a wider population of open models whose true gaps may differ, so the pooled value is their average and should not be read as the behaviour of any one model. Associations with familiarity use Spearman's $\rho$ with a permutation $p$-value. Proportions in the pipeline audit carry Wilson intervals \cite{wilson1927probable}, which are optimistic here because the cuts are nested. The confirmatory study's rules, prompts, events and thresholds were written down and hashed before the data they govern were collected \cite{nosek2018preregistration}, and any incomplete model run is kept out of pooled tests and reported on its own. An earlier version of the exploratory analysis treated items as independent. Switching to event resampling widened the intervals, and all results reported here use it.

\emph{Reproducibility.} Every number in the paper is recomputed from stored result files by scripts in the project repository (see Declarations), and a consistency script checks each quoted value against its source. Pre-registrations and event manifests are hashed. The Project Gutenberg texts are public domain and can be downloaded again using the identifiers listed in the supplementary material. The three web serials are copyrighted and are not redistributed, so the audit on them can only be reproduced by someone who holds the same texts. Timings are wall-clock measurements on the hardware stated; the local and rented GPU figures describe what these experiments cost and are not a controlled hardware benchmark.

None of the models showed a gap from original names: $+4.4$ points [$-2.3$, $+12.0$] for Mistral-Small-3.2-24B, $+0.0$ [$-4.4$, $+4.9$] for OLMo-2-32B and $-0.8$ [$-4.7$, $+2.3$] for Qwen2.5-32B. The confirmatory null and the exploratory finding are not in conflict. The confirmatory sample is mostly made of little-known books (25 of the 32 primary events), and the closed-book probe suggests the open models remember little about them, whereas the exploratory books include several of the best-known novels in English. \emph{Human validation of the oracle.} Figure~\ref{fig:human} summarises the 72 human labels. Both annotators judged the reveal sentence to be an explicit identification in 34 of the 36 validated events (94.4\%, Wilson [81.9, 98.5]\%). In the other two, one or both annotators read it as implied only, and nobody judged that a reveal failed to identify the alias. Every validated event that the model audit had labelled \textsc{explicit}, 28 of 28 (Wilson lower bound 87.9\%), was confirmed as explicit by both annotators. No annotator thought a hidden identity could be named with confidence from the pre-reveal passages (0 of 36 events, Wilson upper bound 9.6\%). At least one annotator did suspect it from clues in 17 of the 36 events, and both did in 8. In 9 events one annotator read the reveal as a reminder of something already said, and in each case the other annotator disagreed. One annotator marked 6 of the 18 validated confirmatory events invalid, and the other accepted all six; no exploratory event was flagged. On the validity and revelation questions almost every answer was the same, which makes $\kappa$ uninformative, so raw agreement (83.3\% and 75.0\%) is the more useful figure there. The annotators knew a book's plot in only 1 of 72 labels. Recomputing the main estimates on the validated events did not change any conclusion. \subsection{Cue-Preserving Controls and Book Familiarity}\label{sec:r-cue}

The letter cipher lowered accuracy on plain controls compared with original names (for Qwen2.5-7B on v2, from 95.4\% to 80.3\%). Part of an original-minus-cipher gap could therefore, in principle, reflect lost reading cues and not memory. Cue-preserving pseudonyms brought control accuracy back to the original level (96.7\% for the same model; all models are listed in the supplementary material), but they did not remove the leakage gap (Figure~\ref{fig:cue} and Table~\ref{tab:cue}). Pooled over the 31 v2 and v3 events for the models that passed the validity gate, the gap was $+14.6$ [$+7.0$, $+22.7$] against the cipher and $+16.1$ [$+8.2$, $+25.0$] against pseudonyms, and the recovered fraction was $-0.11$ [$-0.36$, $+0.06$]. Under the pre-registered decision rule, a recovered fraction below one third counts as memory-dominant, and the entire interval falls below that line, so restoring name cues brings back at most a small part of the gap. The interval includes zero and small negative values. The point estimate should be read as ``close to zero'', not as a sign that pseudonyms increase leakage.

\emph{Familiarity (post hoc).} Once the v6 replication had failed, we looked at whether the gap varies with how well models recognise a book, measured as the number of the five local models that name the identity in the title-assisted probe (Figure~\ref{fig:dose}). \emph{Divergence is not the same as disclosure.} Of the 41 divergent character-cuts, annotator 2 judged 18 to be genuine premature disclosures (2.6\% of 685 cuts, Wilson [1.7, 4.1]\%) and 21 to be already supported by the text up to the cut. With a permissive prompt the rate was 2.0\%, and on the original 289 cuts annotator 1 gave 1.7\% where annotator 2 gave 3.5\%. All three readings rule out zero, and the honest summary is a range of 1.7--3.5\% of character-cuts, not a single number. The two annotators agreed with $\kappa=0.61$ on the 117 characters both labelled and never disagreed about male versus female. Both are models, and one of them implemented the repair.

\emph{Truncation is a sound but incomplete test.} The counterfactual suffix found 47 of the 289 original character-cuts (16.3\%) whose served gender changed when only later chapters changed, compared with the 23 that truncate-and-rebuild detected. The fixes are not free. Evidence-horizon versioning increased the persona stage's attribute rows by 9\% (from 1,074 to 1,172 across the three serials) and raised the share of character-cuts served an \emph{unknown} gender from 39.8\% to 46.4\%. Its wall-clock time went from 6.5\,s to 13.8\,s in the stored single-run measurement, roughly doubling. An earlier single run of the unrepaired stage took 9.1\,s, which would put the increase closer to 50\%, so on this hardware the time cost lies somewhere between one and a half and two times. A versioned card needs one card build for every reader position at which a card is shown, instead of one per book, and cards came out at similar lengths in both builds. Physical truncation costs nothing at inference time, but the system must know where the reader is. A confirmatory pass of 2,220 questions took about five to eight minutes per model on the rented GPU, and the exploratory local runs took 4 to 16 minutes per model and dataset. Per-run timings and API costs are in the supplementary material.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\emph{Why our strongest claim is about pipelines, not memory.} The pipeline results hold across systems, models and leak rules, and the deterministic instruments (truncate-and-rebuild, counterfactual suffixes and canaries) show them with no judge involved. They also follow from the design itself. A stage that reads the whole volume and stamps its output at an earlier position will leak whenever its evidence arrives later than its stamp, whichever model it uses. On the same events, the card experiment put the input channel at about 17 points and the memory-only channel at about 2.5. For assistants working over books like these, what the pipeline feeds the model is a larger risk, and an easier one to control, than what the model remembers.

\emph{Why name-keyed memory looks conditional, and why the null result matters.} The exploratory study shows a strong commercial model naming hidden identities early more often under original names on familiar books, and restoring name cues does not remove the effect. The confirmatory study, run on mostly obscure books with open models that recalled almost none of the identities in a closed-book probe, found no effect. Taken together, the two support a conditional claim: name-keyed premature disclosure appeared when the model plausibly knew the book and did not appear when it plausibly did not. That is why the confirmatory null is worth reporting. It bounds the effect on less familiar material, it shows (together with the unseen-story control) that the harness does not invent gaps, and it keeps the exploratory result from being read as a general property of language models. What remains unproven is the familiarity moderator itself, since the evidence for it is post hoc and the pre-registered test lacked power.

\emph{Engineering implications.} A reader-position marker or a timestamp filter is not enough on its own. The marker failed because the suffix was still in the model's input. The filter failed because the decision about what to filter had already been made using the suffix. Both points hold beyond the systems we tested as arguments about design, even if the specific rates do not. The repairs that worked all apply one rule, that anything visible at $c$ is computed from $P(c)$ alone, at four different places: derived facts (evidence-horizon versioning), summaries (prefix-built cards), model input (physical truncation) and indexing (prefix-causal indexes). The counterfactual-suffix result adds a lesson about testing. Truncate-and-rebuild on a single volume misses any dependence that happens to agree with the book, so a system meant to be faithful should be tested with altered suffixes as well as removed ones.

\emph{Costs and limits of the mitigations.} Reader-faithfulness and correctness are different goals. Versioning withheld values that the prefix supported, the anchored cue that recovered them did not transfer to another corpus, and pseudonymisation reduced disclosure only by reducing accuracy. The repairs worked in our tests, but that is not a formal guarantee. Definition~\ref{def:rf} ranges over every possible suffix, whereas our tests cover a removed suffix and one pronoun swap, on one codebase and a small set of models. A guarantee for arbitrary books, pipelines and models would need faithfulness to be built in by construction and verified against the code.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}\label{sec:limits}

\emph{Construct validity.} An explicit reveal sentence marks when the book states an identity, not when a reader could first infer it. Premature naming against this oracle can therefore include reasonable inference from clues. Because this affects every arm equally, it cannot create a gap by itself, but it does blur what ``premature'' means for a single item. In the human check nobody judged an identity confidently inferable before the reveal, yet at least one annotator suspected it in 17 of 36 events. In about half of the events, then, the reveal sentence confirms what earlier clues have already hinted at, and ``premature'' should be read as ``before the book states it''.

\emph{Event-selection bias.} Language models proposed and audited every event. Two of them, the confirmatory miner and auditor, were also among the models evaluated, and selection was restricted to reveals stated in sentences that match fixed cue patterns. Some mined events are of poor quality. Seven of the 68 confirmatory events have a descriptive phrase as their ``identity'', and in the human check one annotator judged 6 of the 18 validated confirmatory events invalid, although the other annotator accepted them. The events are therefore not a representative sample of concealed identities in fiction, and the effects could differ for implicit, gradual or visual reveals.

\emph{External validity.} The corpora are public-domain Western fiction, three translated web serials and one synthetic volume. The models are five small local models, three open 24--32B models and one commercial reference model. The results do not establish rates for contemporary serial fiction, other languages, other genres or other assistant architectures.

\emph{Annotation validity.} The human check covers 36 public-domain events, with two author-annotators per event. The annotators did not see model labels but did know the study's hypotheses, and their validity judgements disagreed on six events, so the check bounds event quality without settling it. Outside that check, every non-deterministic label comes from a model: the disclosure labels in the pipeline audit (two model annotators, one of which implemented the repair; $\kappa=0.61$), the card judge rule and the selection-leak judge. The judge was validated against a deterministic rule only on the events where that rule applies. Model judgements about whether a divergence is a genuine spoiler have no independent human check, because the serials could not be shared.

\emph{Statistical power.} The confirmatory primary set has 32 events, only seven of them from mid- or high-fame books, and the familiarity contrast could detect only differences of about 13 points. The selection test rests on 24 merged events. Intervals in the pipeline audit are optimistic because the cuts are nested within three serials.

\emph{Implementation dependence.} The pipeline audit concerns one narrative graph's persona stage, one card prompt, one long-context layout and one resolver design. The rates belong to those implementations. The mechanisms and the tests should carry over to other systems; the numbers need not.

\emph{Reproducibility and access.} The public-domain texts, prompts and analysis code are available. The commercial reference model, however, may change or be withdrawn behind its API, model files may be updated under the same name, and the serial texts cannot be redistributed. Exact model outputs therefore cannot be guaranteed to reproduce, even with the stored prompts and seeds, whereas the reported statistics can be reproduced from the stored outputs.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}

We defined reader-faithfulness as non-interference from the unread part of a book and evaluated it in two ways: a reveal-time evaluation of parametric memory, and an audit of narrative pipelines and generic assistant designs. The reference model's name-keyed memory effect appeared in the exploratory setting but was not confirmed in the pre-registered study, whose books were mostly unfamiliar to the models. It is best understood as conditional on familiarity, which still needs to be tested directly. Pipeline processing that depends on future text, on the other hand, produced measurable leakage in every design we tested.

The main lesson for engineering is that a reader-position marker or a timestamp filter cannot make an assistant reader-faithful if upstream derivations, cards or retrieval decisions have already used later text. Building from the prefix and versioning facts by their evidence horizon substantially reduced the forms of leakage we tested, at the costs we report and within the limits of this evaluation.

Three directions stand out for future work: extending human validation from the reveal oracle to disclosure judgements on serial text that can be shared, running a prospective test that varies familiarity using matched famous and obscure books, and enforcing evidence horizons by construction in a pipeline so that faithfulness can be checked against the code instead of only measured.
