\title[Maintaining Reader-Faithfulness in AI Narrative Assistants]{Maintaining Reader-Faithfulness in AI Narrative Assistants: Measuring Parametric Memory Leakage and Future-Information Leakage in Processing Pipelines}

\author[1]{\fnm{Adhyan} \sur{Jain}}\email{adhyan.jain2024@vitstudent.ac.in}

\author[1]{\fnm{Atharva Sheersh} \sur{Pandey}}\email{atharva.sheersh2024@vitstudent.ac.in}

\author[1]{\fnm{Aryan} \sur{Gahlot}}\email{aryan.gahlot2024@vitstudent.ac.in}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Shashank Mouli} \sur{Satapathy}}\email{shashankmouli.s@vit.ac.in}

\affil[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Computer Science and Engineering}, \orgname{Vellore Institute of Technology (VIT)}, \orgaddress{\city{Vellore}, \postcode{632014}, \state{Tamil Nadu}, \country{India}}}

\abstract{When an AI reading assistant summarizes a story, it must avoid spoiling plot points the book has not yet revealed. Existing work focuses on whether models memorize books; this study asks whether models leak character identities before their reveal, or let future chapters influence earlier output. We define reader-faithfulness as non-interference from the unread text and evaluate two leakage channels. In a pre-registered confirmatory study, three open models showed no original-name gap in premature naming (+0.1 points, 95\% CI \textminus5.0 to +5.2), and the exploratory gap of a commercial reference model (+26.0 points) was not reproduced. A component-level audit of a persona stage found that its output diverged from a prefix-only build in 41 of 685 character-cuts; evidence-horizon versioning produced zero observed divergence in the tested configurations. In generic-assistant experiments, whole-volume processing caused content leakage, and future-informed entity merging produced a 19.4\% model-judged disclosure rate against 1.4\% for a prefix-causal index. A long-context assistant told the reader's position repeated a planted suffix token in 40.6\% of events, whereas physical truncation produced zero observed suffix-canary hits in the tested configuration. Events were model-selected, disclosure labels are model-judged with limited independent human validation, and three nested serials cannot support population-level estimates.}

\keywords{Reader-faithfulness, Non-interference, Information-flow control, Spoilers, Parametric memory, Narrative understanding}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Imagine you're halfway through a mystery novel and you ask an AI assistant, ``Who is the masked guest?'' Since the book doesn't reveal the answer until the final chapters, the assistant shouldn't spoil it for you. Today, language models are constantly used to summarize long novels, answer reader questions, and maintain character guides for serial fiction. Every one of these tasks relies on a specific reader position: if you are on chapter $c$, the AI should only draw on what the first $c$ chapters say. We call a system \emph{reader-faithful} when its output at a given position does not depend on the rest of the book. While the concept is simple to state, the experiments below show that it is easy to break without anyone noticing.

An assistant can break reader-faithfulness in two primary ways. The first is \emph{parametric memory}. If a model was trained on text that includes the book you're reading, it might already know who the masked guest is. In this case, simply cutting its input down to the chapters you've read does not stop it from stating the answer.

The second way is through \emph{future information introduced by the pipeline}. If a preprocessing stage, a character card, an entity index, or a long-context prompt was built using the entire book, those later chapters can shape what the AI tells you early on, even if the individual model call only sees your current reading prefix. While the direct memorization of books has been studied extensively \cite{chang2023speak,fu2026summarization}, and renaming characters is a standard trick to control for it \cite{jiang2024beyond,deusser2026anonymization}, the pipeline channel has received far less attention. It closely resembles look-ahead bias in financial forecasting \cite{glasserman2023lookahead} or the challenge of point-in-time correctness in stream processing \cite{akidau2015dataflow}. Based on our literature review (described in Section~\ref{sec:rw-spoiler}), we found no prior studies measuring either of these channels specifically as premature disclosure at a reader position.

This question initially arose while we were building a reading assistant for long web serials, but we approach it here purely as a measurement problem. Previous work leaves three gaps. First, memorization studies typically score recall (like name cloze tests or summary quality) rather than checking if a mid-book answer improperly reveals future text. Second, renaming studies show that performance drops when names change, but they fail to separate true memory tied to a specific name from the loss of standard reading cues, such as a character's gender. Finally, while temporal and spoiler-aware narrative systems maintain chapter-safe states, none test whether a representation derived at one position changes when only the unread text changes. These gaps lead us to three core research questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} Under what conditions does a model reveal a character's identity before the explicit narrative reveal, and how does this behavior change when using original names, renamed characters, pseudonyms, or varying book familiarity?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} How much future information leaks into a reader-positioned character state via derived facts, whole-volume character cards, and long-context inputs, and which repairs reduce this leakage?
\item \textbf{RQ3.} Can entity resolution or retrieval decisions made with future information spoil a hidden identity, even if the passages shown to the reader strictly come from the prefix?
\end{itemize}

This paper makes three main contributions. First, for RQ1, we introduce a \emph{reveal-time evaluation protocol}: we use the book's own explicit reveal sentence to pinpoint exactly when an identity becomes known. By using renaming strategies alongside cue-preserving controls, we can isolate name-keyed memory from lost reading cues across both exploratory and pre-registered confirmatory phases. Second, for RQ2 and RQ3, we conduct an \emph{audit of future-dependent narrative pipelines} (using truncate-and-rebuild, counterfactual suffixes, and suffix canaries) to distinguish genuine pipeline dependence from true disclosure. Finally, we provide a \emph{comparison of mitigations and their measured costs}, evaluating each against the naive repairs they replace.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work and Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the problem formally. Sections~\ref{sec:method} and \ref{sec:setup} describe the methods and the experimental setup, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}, \ref{sec:limits} and \ref{sec:conclusion} discuss them, list the limitations and conclude.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{LLM Memorisation and Book Familiarity}\label{sec:rw-mem}

Chang et al.\ \cite{chang2023speak} used a name-cloze membership query to estimate which books ChatGPT and GPT-4 had memorised. Memorisation tracked how often a book's passages appear on the web, and the models did better on downstream tasks for books they had memorised. Fu et al.\ \cite{fu2026summarization} compared summaries a model writes from memory with summaries it writes from the full text; the full text usually, though not always, produced the better summary. Studies of benchmark contamination reach similar conclusions about evaluation data \cite{sainz2023contamination,golchin2024timetravel}, and extraction studies measure verbatim memorisation \cite{carlini2019secret,carlini2023quantifying}. These studies show that models partly know familiar books and measure that knowledge through recall, cloze accuracy or summary quality. We measure whether an answer given at a reader position names an identity the text has not yet revealed, when the only justified answer is that the identity is still undetermined. A model could do well on name cloze and still keep a secret, or the other way round, so our outcome does not follow from theirs. We carry the familiarity question over to premature disclosure and do not set out to establish book memorisation itself.

\subsection{Renaming and Memorisation-Constrained Evaluation}\label{sec:rw-rename}

Renaming characters is a well-established way to limit memorisation in evaluation. Jiang and Ferraro \cite{jiang2024beyond} replaced character names while keeping implicit cues and saw accuracy on popular fiction fall from 96\% to 72\%, with drops of up to 18\% on character-understanding tasks. Gu et al.\ \cite{gu2026synthworlds} built parallel worlds, one mapped to real entities and one synthetic, and measured how much real-world knowledge helps. Deußer et al.\ \cite{deusser2026anonymization} studied anonymisation as a privacy--utility trade-off over eleven benchmarks and found that reversible methods which keep entities distinct cost less than redaction. We use renaming in this standard sense, applied to a reveal-time outcome, and add a cue-preserving pseudonym arm because a letter cipher removes cues such as gender and name type along with the memorised name. If pseudonyms bring ordinary reading accuracy back while the advantage of original names remains, the most consistent explanation is memory keyed to the specific name. Any transformation can change other properties of a text, so other explanations remain possible, and we present this as an inference and not as a demonstrated mechanism.

\subsection{Reader-Position-Aware Narrative State and Coreference}\label{sec:rw-state}

Several systems track narrative state over time. TimeChara \cite{ahn2024timechara} evaluates point-in-time hallucination in role-playing models and lists spoiler avoidance among its motivations, but its errors are contradictions of a character's timeline, not disclosures tied to a reveal. Ma et al.\ \cite{ma2026script} benchmark whether interactive narrators built from movie synopses keep their commitments over long interactions. Writer-memory systems store chapter-safe or belief-scoped story state. The Narrative World Model \cite{saifullah2026nwm} reads every system through chapter-safe evidence and separates the order of events from the narration that reveals them, and NarraWorld \cite{tian2026narraworld} keeps a belief view for each character. E$^2$RAG \cite{zhang2025e2rag} holds entities and events in separate subgraphs to keep retrieval over novels temporally and causally consistent. These are the closest existing designs, and they differ in what they enforce. As far as their published descriptions show, the Narrative World Model comes closest to a reader-visible evidence horizon, because every read goes through chapter-safe evidence. TimeChara and E$^2$RAG track or score consistency with story time, Ma et al.\ score consistency of commitments over an interaction, and NarraWorld keeps per-character belief views, which is a different notion from what a reader has been told. None of them tests whether a stage computed with access to later chapters changes what is served at an earlier position. Tracking story time, character beliefs or narrative consistency does not by itself stop a stage from reading later chapters; only an enforced reader-visible evidence horizon does, and we found no published test of that property.

The entity layer of such systems usually comes from literary coreference resources (Table~\ref{tab:rw}). Their labels are omniscient: a mention is linked to whoever it refers to in the finished book. KoCoNovel \cite{kim2024koconovel} is the exception, since it also releases a version annotated from the reader's perspective. Before the reveal, an alias correctly merged with its true identity is exactly the merge a reader-faithful system must keep hidden. Resolution quality and reader-faithfulness are therefore different properties, and the audit in Section~\ref{sec:m-pipeline} measures the second.

\subsection{Temporal Retrieval and Spoiler-Safe Assistants}\label{sec:rw-spoiler}

Research on spoilers has mostly addressed detecting them in reviews \cite{wan2019spoiler} rather than stopping an assistant from producing them. Assistants usually ground their answers in a book through retrieval-augmented generation \cite{lewis2020rag}, and long-context models can now read a whole volume at once, although how much they use a piece of information depends on where it sits in the input \cite{liu2024lost}. Some products already try to be spoiler-safe. One database vendor documents a pattern that stamps each chapter with a synthetic time and filters recall by the reader's position, and notes that passages are not fully gated by that filter \cite{surrealdb2026spoiler}. A reading companion is described as giving the model only the chapters the reader has finished \cite{aiindigo2026paige}. We cite these vendor descriptions only to show that position filtering and input truncation exist as designs; they are not evaluations, and product documentation is not evidence that either technique prevents spoilers. Our method borrows two ideas. Attaching to each value the latest input time it depends on comes from watermarking in stream processing \cite{akidau2015dataflow}, and planting a unique token and searching outputs for it comes from memorisation testing \cite{carlini2019secret}. What is new is their application to reader positions, and the measurements in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Our literature and product searches, logged in the project repository between 15 and 23 October 2026, turned up no study that measures selection leakage, uses suffix canaries at a reader position, or audits a narrative graph by truncation and rebuilding. This statement covers our search only and is not a claim that no such work exists.

\section{Problem Formulation and Reader-Faithfulness}\label{sec:problem}

To set this up formally, think of any given book $B$ as a collection of sequential pieces, denoted as $(b_1,\dots,b_n)$. These pieces could be individual chapters or even raw character offsets. When a person reads up to a certain point $c$, everything they have consumed so far forms the prefix $P(c)=(b_1,\dots,b_c)$, leaving the rest of the text as the unseen suffix. A narrative assistant has two parts. Its \emph{fixed configuration} is everything that does not come from the book: model weights and version, prompts, decoding parameters and algorithm settings. Its \emph{state-construction process} turns the book into everything else the assistant serves from: knowledge graphs, derived facts, character cards, entity mappings, indexes, caches and persistent state. This second kind of state is book-derived and can itself depend on the unread suffix, so a comparison must not hold it fixed. When the input book changes from $B$ to $B'$, the configuration stays the same and all book-derived state is rebuilt from $B'$. Write $F(B,c)$ for the assistant's output at position $c$ under that rule. It covers the final response and every intermediate representation the response can draw on, including derived facts, cards, entity-merge decisions and the set of retrieved passages.

\begin{definition}[Reader-faithfulness]\label{def:rf}
A component $X$ of $F$ is reader-faithful at position $c$ if $F_X(B,c)=F_X(B',c)$ for every book $B'$ whose prefix at $c$ equals $P(c)$.
\end{definition}

\emph{Observable boundary and equality.} A test can only compare what it can observe, so we fix the boundary of $F$ as the values that cross a defined interface towards the reader, together with the intermediate representations logged at named interfaces inside the pipeline. For each component, equality is decided on that component's own observable value: for a derived trait, exact equality of the categorical value the reader query returns for a character, trait key and position (so ``unknown'' differs from ``male''); for an entity-merge decision, equality of the Boolean merge outcome for an alias--identity pair; for retrieval, equality of the set of served passage identifiers; and for free text, such as a card or an answer, a projection onto disclosure instead of byte equality, namely whether a planted suffix token appears (exact string match) or whether the text identifies the alias with the identity (text or judge rule, Section~\ref{sec:m-mitigation}). Internal state outside these interfaces, such as model activations, caches or log order, is outside the boundary. A difference inside the boundary is a violation of Definition~\ref{def:rf} for that component; a byte-level difference in free text that changes no projection is not counted.

Put simply, $F(B,c)$ is what the assistant serves a reader at position $c$ of the whole book, and $F(P(c),c)$ is what it serves when it has only ever seen the prefix. Changing the unread suffix must not change what is served at $c$, which makes this a non-interference criterion. Two practical tests follow. If $B'$ is the prefix on its own, we get the \emph{truncate-and-rebuild} test, $F(B,c)=F(P(c),c)$. If $B'$ is the book with a rewritten suffix, we get the \emph{counterfactual-suffix} test. Both are necessary consequences of Definition~\ref{def:rf}. Neither is sufficient, since each checks only one alternative suffix. Because the definition quantifies over every suffix, however, a single observed difference between $F(B,c)$ and $F(P(c),c)$ already shows that the component is not reader-faithful, without any sampling inference. Statistics matter only for the separate question of how often a violation occurs. The definition is also not a test of correctness: a faithful system can still be wrong, and a difference between $F(B,c)$ and $F(P(c),c)$ need not reveal anything a reader could not already work out.

Three qualifications govern how the definition is used. First, for a stochastic model, equality of two single outputs is not a complete criterion; the exact condition is equality of the output distributions. Every experiment here uses temperature 0 and fixed seeds, which makes the reported tests operationally reproducible and lets us compare single outputs; it does not establish equality of output distributions, and residual nondeterminism, such as GPU kernels or a vendor model that changes behind its API, is not controlled. Second, the definition ranges over all suffixes that share the prefix, whereas the experiments test a removed suffix and a few chosen counterfactual suffixes. They are sampled operational tests of reader-faithfulness, not a verification method and not proofs that the property holds for all books and suffixes. Third, one audit deliberately departs from the rule that book-derived state is rebuilt. Truncate-and-rebuild starts both builds from the same stored assignment of mentions to entities, which is book-derived state held fixed in order to isolate the persona stage; it would hide a dependence located in entity resolution. That audit is therefore a \emph{component-level} test of one stage, not an end-to-end rebuild of the whole system from the prefix, and its results describe that stage only. To keep this design from concealing such a dependence, we test the entity-merge decision directly in the selection experiment (Section~\ref{sec:m-mitigation}) and check that the resolver changes no entity under truncation (Section~\ref{sec:r-pipeline}).

For this reason we evaluate two separate things. \emph{Behavioural leakage} is an actual disclosure of a hidden identity or future fact before the book states it. \emph{Pipeline dependence} is any change in the output, or in an intermediate representation, when only the unread suffix changes. Dependence can be measured without labels. A change in an internal representation does not by itself mean that a spoiler has been disclosed; deciding whether a particular dependence amounts to a disclosure means judging what the prefix supports, which is why the audit in Section~\ref{sec:m-pipeline} reports both. We keep the two apart in every figure, table and conclusion that follows. We also evaluate faithfulness together with utility, since a repair that withholds every uncertain value is faithful and useless, and we report each mitigation below with both what it removes and what it costs the reader.

Parametric memory works differently. A model whose training data included $B$ can name a hidden identity from the prefix alone; $F(P(c),c)$ then leaks by itself, and truncation does not help. We measure this channel through behaviour. In an \emph{event} $e$, an alias (``the Opera ghost'') is later identified with a named character (``Erik'') by an explicit sentence of the book, which we call the \emph{reveal}. The \emph{cutoff} is a position before the reveal. Each \emph{pre-reveal item} shows a passage that ends before the cutoff, marks one mention of the alias, lists the event's cast plus the option \textsc{undetermined}, and asks who the marked expression refers to. The only justified answer is \textsc{undetermined}. Giving the true identity is \emph{premature naming}. Let $y_{ei}^{a}\in\{0,1\}$ record premature naming on item $i$ of event $e$ under arm $a$, where the arms are original names ($A$), the mean of two independent letter ciphers ($B$), and cue-preserving pseudonyms ($B3$). The \emph{leakage gap} is the difference in premature-naming proportions, in percentage points:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap}
\Delta_{AB}=p_A-p_B,\qquad
p_a^{\mathrm{item}}=\frac{\sum_e\sum_{i=1}^{n_e}y_{ei}^{a}}{\sum_e n_e},\qquad
p_a^{\mathrm{event}}=\frac{1}{E}\sum_{e=1}^{E}\frac{1}{n_e}\sum_{i=1}^{n_e}y_{ei}^{a}.
\end{equation}
The exploratory analyses use the item-pooled proportion $p^{\mathrm{item}}$, which gives more weight to events with more pre-reveal items. The pre-registered confirmatory analysis uses the event-weighted proportion $p^{\mathrm{event}}$, where each event counts once. In both cases, uncertainty comes from resampling events, never items (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Throughout the paper, ``leakage gap'' means $\Delta_{AB}$, the original-name proportion minus the mean of the two cipher arms; every other contrast, such as $\Delta_{AB3}$, the whole-volume-minus-prefix card contrast or the familiarity contrast P1, is named explicitly. The \emph{recovered fraction} $\rho=(p_{B3}-p_B)/(p_A-p_B)$ is the share of the gap that comes back when name cues are restored. A value near 1 means the gap was about lost cues; a value near 0 means it is tied to the particular names. Because the denominator is the gap itself, $\rho$ is unstable and hard to interpret when $p_A-p_B$ is close to zero, and we read it only for pools in which that gap is clearly positive.

The pipeline audit needs four more terms, each used only in the sense given here. A \emph{character-cut} is a pair (character, cut position $k$) for which both the full build and the prefix build serve a value of some derived trait at reader position $k$. A \emph{divergent fact} is a character-cut where the two builds serve different values. A fact is \emph{exposed} at $k$ when it is stamped at or before $k$ but its \emph{evidence horizon}, the latest position of the text it was computed from, lies after $k$. A \emph{genuine disclosure} is a divergent fact whose full-build value the text up to $k$ does not support. A divergence whose value the prefix already supports is dependence without disclosure.

We use the following terms consistently. \emph{Premature naming} is the behavioural outcome of the memory task. \emph{Pipeline dependence} is a change in an internal representation when only the suffix changes. \emph{Genuine disclosure} is a dependence that discloses something the prefix does not support. \emph{Leakage} without a qualifier is reserved for behavioural, content or selection leakage, never for a change in an internal representation alone. Content and selection leakage are the two forms of future information that a pipeline introduces. In a \emph{content leak}, text from the suffix reaches the model's input. In a \emph{selection leak}, the model sees only prefix text, but the choice of which prefix text to serve, or which entities to merge, was made using the suffix. Figure~\ref{fig:channels} sets out the three channels, the evaluation that detects each one and the place where each mitigation acts.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

The study has two experimental tracks, both built on the notions of a reader position and an evidence horizon (Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}). Track 1 measures parametric memory using reveal-time events from public-domain novels (Sections~\ref{sec:m-corpus} and \ref{sec:m-memory}). Track 2 audits a narrative knowledge graph and several generic assistant designs for dependence on future text (Sections~\ref{sec:m-pipeline} and \ref{sec:m-mitigation}); its generic-assistant experiments reuse the events from Track 1.

\subsection{Corpus and Reveal-Time Event Construction}\label{sec:m-corpus}

Every event comes from a Project Gutenberg plain-text novel \cite{gutenberg} first published before 1928. We remove the Project Gutenberg header and footer (everything before the \texttt{*** START} marker and after the \texttt{*** END} marker) and strip carriage returns; the text is otherwise unchanged. Matching ignores whitespace differences: each sentence becomes a regular expression in which any run of whitespace matches any other, so line wrapping in the plain text has no effect.

An event consists of a book, one or more alias surfaces, an identity name, a cast list of seven to nine names that includes the identity, a verbatim reveal sentence and a cutoff anchor. The reveal sentence has to occur exactly once in the book; if it occurs twice or not at all, the event is dropped rather than disambiguated. The cutoff is placed at the start of the nearest chapter heading before the reveal, taken as the last match of the heading pattern that begins before the reveal, because tables of contents repeat the headings. If the alias never appears before that heading, a fixed phrase at least 200 characters before the reveal serves as the cutoff instead. A deterministic verifier then checks five conditions: the cutoff comes before the reveal; the alias appears at least three times before the cutoff; no sentence before the cutoff contains both an alias surface and a capitalised token of the identity name; the identity and the aliases share no capitalised token, so the name itself gives nothing away; and the prefix mentions the cast often enough to build the plain controls of Section~\ref{sec:m-memory}.

Language models propose candidate events and these rules verify them. For the exploratory sets (PD v2, v3 and v6), a model drafter proposed events one book at a time. For the confirmatory set, a pre-registered pipeline searched sentences of books sampled by fame band for a fixed list of reveal cues (for example ``was none other than'', ``under the assumed name'' and ``revealed himself''). An open model then extracted the alias, the identity and the reveal sentence, and the reveal sentence had to appear verbatim in the passage. An auditor model from a different family labelled each reveal \textsc{explicit}, \textsc{implied} or \textsc{not supported} with respect to the claim that the alias is the identity, and the \textsc{explicit} events form the confirmatory primary set. The commercial reference model later audited the exploratory events and marked 20 of the 31 v2 and v3 events \textsc{explicit}, 2 \textsc{implied} and 9 \textsc{not supported}.

The result is a \emph{reproducible, text-grounded oracle with model-assisted event selection}. Once a model has proposed an event, matching and cutoff verification are deterministic, so anyone with the same texts and rules gets the same items and labels. This does not make the choice of events objective: which events exist in the first place, and whether a given reveal sentence really identifies the alias, depend on model judgements. The oracle also records only what the book \emph{states}: it identifies the first selected explicit reveal sentence under the rules above, not the first point at which a knowledgeable reader could infer the identity.

Some mined identities are descriptive phrases rather than names (``a Greek'', ``the son of a long-descended English peer''). They stay in every primary analysis. The post hoc sensitivity analyses that drop them use one fixed rule, applied to every analysis that uses the confirmatory events: an identity string whose first word is an article or a pronoun (\emph{the, a, an, him, his, her, one}) counts as descriptive. The rule can be re-applied to the identity strings in the event manifest (supplementary material) and is implemented in \texttt{scripts/paper/posthoc\_descriptive\_identity.py}.

To check the oracle, three of the authors labelled a stratified, seeded subset of 36 events: 18 exploratory events spread across the three datasets, and 18 from the confirmatory primary set that include every high- and mid-fame event. Two annotators labelled each event independently, without seeing any model label, in an annotation tool that shows the reveal sentence in context alongside the same pre-reveal passages the models received. For each event they recorded whether they already knew the book's plot, whether the reveal sentence identifies the alias explicitly, only by implication or not at all, whether it reads as a revelation or as a reminder of something already said, whether a first-time reader of the earlier passages could name the identity (no, suspect or confident), and whether the event is valid. We summarise agreement with Fleiss' $\kappa$ \cite{fleiss1971measuring} and recompute the main gaps after removing every event that either annotator flagged. The rubric distinguishes four kinds of event, illustrated below with events from the check. An \emph{explicit} event has a reveal passage from which a reader of that passage alone learns that alias and identity are one person; in \emph{Oliver Twist} the reveal states that Monks is Edward Leeford, and both annotators labelled it explicit and not inferable earlier. An \emph{implied} event leaves that link to inference; in \emph{The Red-Headed League} the passage lets the reader conclude, without stating it, that Vincent Spaulding is John Clay, and both annotators labelled it implied. An \emph{already inferable} event is one where the earlier passages let a careful first-time reader suspect or name the identity, so the reveal confirms rather than discloses; in \emph{King Solomon's Mines} both annotators suspected from earlier clues that Umbopa is Ignosi. An \emph{invalid} event is not a reference to a hidden person with a specific named identity; one annotator marked invalid the \emph{Dracula's Guest} event, whose alias is the generic description ``a fisherman'' (for Saft Tammie), and a confirmatory event whose alias is the first-person pronoun ``I'' (no reason was recorded; the other annotator accepted both). The full rubric and every event-level label are in the supplementary material. Although blind to model labels, the annotators are authors and knew the study's hypotheses. The check bounds the quality of the oracle on this sample; it does not show that the event-selection procedure is unbiased or that every event in the corpus is valid. Its results appear in Section~\ref{sec:r-memory}.

\subsection{Parametric-Memory Intervention and Controls}\label{sec:m-memory}

Each pre-reveal item shows the last 1,200 characters of text up to the end of the sentence containing the sampled alias mention, with the mention wrapped in double brackets. We sample up to eight pre-reveal mentions per event (four in the confirmatory study), evenly spaced over the prefix, so items sit anywhere from a few hundred to several hundred thousand characters before the cutoff. The candidate list is the event's cast in a seeded random order plus \textsc{undetermined}, and the model replies with a single letter, held to the allowed set by a JSON schema where the runtime supports one. Two kinds of control item are built the same way. \emph{Post-reveal items} are alias mentions after the reveal, where the identity is the right answer; they check that the task can be answered at all. \emph{Plain controls} are mentions of ordinary cast members before the cutoff; they measure how well the model reads the passage under each arm.

The arms differ only in names. Arm A shows the original text. Arm B replaces every proper-noun token, meaning any capitalised token of three or more letters that never appears in lowercase in the book, with a case- and length-preserving letter substitution drawn from a fixed per-book seed. Honorifics and other protected words stay as they are, substitutions that would collide with real words are redrawn, and the same mapping is applied to the window, the marked mention and the candidate list, so the correct letter is the same in every arm. Arm B2 repeats B with a second, independent seed, and the analysis averages B and B2, so that a gap produced by only one cipher does not count. Arm B3 keeps the cues a cipher destroys: it replaces the cast and alias name tokens with natural pseudonyms matched for gender (inferred from the pronouns that follow the name in the book) and for name type (given name or surname), and leaves every other proper noun under the arm-B cipher so that places and bystanders still offer no memory cue. Because answers are letters, an answer maps back to an identity by lookup in the event's candidate list. Window text and candidate order are the same across arms. Two probes test memory directly. The closed-book probe asks who the alias is without giving any passage, and the title-assisted probe gives only the book title and the alias. Familiarity in Section~\ref{sec:r-cue} is the number of five local models that name the identity under the title-assisted probe. Before any gap is interpreted, each model must pass a validity gate on each dataset: the two ciphers must agree on at least 90\% of plain controls, and the original and cipher arms must agree on at least 80\% of items from synthetic stories with invented names, where memory cannot play a part. The gate is applied to each model separately on each exploratory dataset. A model that fails is dropped only from the ``models passing the gate'' pool and is never removed from the per-model results; the reference model, a positive control, is always reported on its own. On v2 and v3, Gemma-2-9B, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B and Phi-4-mini passed. Qwen2.5-7B failed the first criterion on both sets (its two ciphers agreed on 80.9\% and 83.3\% of plain controls), as did the reference model (89.5\% and 85.4\%); the ``all six models'' row of the pooled cue-preserving results (Section~\ref{sec:r-cue}) shows the effect of keeping them. On v6 the same gate was applied, with the synthetic-story agreement taken from each model's v3 run because v6 has no synthetic items of its own; Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B and Phi-4-mini passed, while Gemma-2-9B (85.7\% agreement between ciphers), Qwen2.5-7B (87.5\%) and the reference model (83.9\%) did not. The v6 result for the local pool therefore rests on three models, not four. When pseudonyms restore control accuracy but the gap stays, we describe name-keyed memory as the most consistent explanation and do not claim to have shown the mechanism inside the model. Natural pseudonyms may still carry linguistic associations beyond gender and name type, such as cultural origin or register, so arm B3 is not a perfect control for every semantic property of the original names.

\subsection{Pipeline Audit and Counterfactual Suffix Tests}\label{sec:m-pipeline}

The audited system is a narrative knowledge graph built for long web serials. It processes a volume as a whole and gives every fact three time axes: discourse order, story time, and \emph{knowledge time}, the position from which a reader could know the fact. Its reader query returns, for a character and a position $c$, the most recent value of each trait stamped at or before $c$. Because the whole volume is processed together, the moment a fact is written says nothing about when a reader could know it, so knowledge time has to be reconstructed from where the evidence is. We audit the \emph{persona stage}, which derives a character's gender, age band, speech register and archetype from the first twelve chapters in which the character appears, and stamps each trait at the chapter of first appearance. Suppose a character first appears in chapter 1 but their pronouns only show up in chapter 3. A query at chapter 2 is then served a gender that a build over chapters 1 and 2 could not contain.

\emph{Truncate-and-rebuild.} For each cut $k$, we run the persona stage twice: once on the full volume, and once on a copy of the database with every chapter, span, mention and segment after $k$ deleted. Both runs start from the same stored assignment of mentions to entities, which separates the persona stage from entity resolution; this is a component-level audit of the persona stage, not an end-to-end rebuild of the system. Holding resolution fixed isolates the persona stage but cannot detect a dependence located in the entity-merge decisions themselves; the selection experiment of Section~\ref{sec:m-mitigation} tests that case, and a separate check (Section~\ref{sec:r-pipeline}) found no entity change in the resolver when later chapters were removed. We then compare the values served at position $k+0.5$ for every character that has a persona fact in both builds. The audit covers three translated web serials, \emph{Lord of the Mysteries}, \emph{Omniscient Reader's Viewpoint} and \emph{Reverend Insanity}. Thirteen cuts yield 289 character-cuts and eleven further cuts add 396. Cuts are nested within each serial, so character-cuts are not independent: the counts describe these three serials, and the intervals around them are optimistic and should not be read as samples from a wider population of serials.

\emph{Static exposure screen.} We read each trait's evidence horizon from the stage's code (gender, for instance, reads the first twelve attested chapters) and flag a fact as exposed at $k$ when its stamp is at most $k$ and its horizon is beyond $k$. The screen shows where divergence is possible without any rebuild. Because the dependency table was taken from the audited code itself, the screen is close to sound by construction; we use it to mark the cuts at which the stage's code allows no divergence, not to predict which ones diverge.

\emph{Disclosure labels.} A divergence alone does not mean a reader has been spoiled. For every divergent character-cut, two model annotators judged whether the text up to the cut supported the full-build value. Annotator 1 was a language-model session that also took part in implementing the repair; it wrote its labels without seeing any system value, and the labels were frozen by hash. Annotator 2 was the commercial reference model, run with a fixed prompt and a quote check that accepts an answer only if at least 80\% of its quoted words appear in order in the cited passage. A third pass with a more permissive prompt gives the other end of the range. Every table and figure keeps these model-based labels separate from the deterministic divergence measure, which needs no labels. Because annotator 1 also took part in implementing the repair, its labels carry a risk of evaluator bias despite the hash freeze and the blinding to system values, so we do not present the model-annotated disclosure rate as independently validated. The small human check described next bounds how far the labels can be trusted.

\emph{Human check of the disclosure labels.} We drew 48 character-cuts, plus four repeats to measure self-consistency, from six strata fixed before anyone annotated: divergent cuts that the models judged to be genuine disclosures, divergent cuts judged supported by the prefix, cuts where the model annotators disagreed, the reverse direction, cuts where the repair withholds a value, and non-divergent controls. The seed and the manifest hash were recorded in advance. The first three authors labelled the cases in a blind web form, two per case in rotation. Each saw only the passages up to the cut (at most eight per case) and first said whether those passages establish the character as male, female or neither, and how confident they were. The served value was shown only after that answer, and annotators then judged whether the passages support it and whether a first-time reader would plausibly hold it. They also recorded whether they already knew the book. The serial excerpts stayed inside the project. The sample is purposive: it bounds how far the model labels agree with people in each stratum but does not estimate how common disclosure is. The guideline told annotators to judge only the passages shown and said that a name which sounds male or female is not a signal. The raw annotator pairs are the primary evidence for agreement. Where one label per case is needed, the adjudication rule is stated with the results, because the first author's labels were partly name-based and the adjudication by a third annotator that the protocol foresaw was not available. This 48-cut check of pipeline disclosure answers a different question from the 36-event check of reveal explicitness in Section~\ref{sec:m-corpus}, and the two are never combined.

\emph{Counterfactual suffix.} We keep chapters up to $k$ byte-identical, swap the gendered pronouns in every later span, rebuild the whole volume and check whether the value served at $k$ moves. This measures dependence rather than divergence. It needs no truncation or labels and works for closed-vocabulary outputs, where a planted string could never appear.

Two further checks probe the mechanism and how far it generalises. The first uses a synthetic volume of 40 chapters and 80 characters, with known genders and a known chapter in which each character's own pronoun cue first appears, generated in nine configurations of cue density with five seeds each. The second runs two English public-domain novels, \emph{Oliver Twist} and \emph{The Three Musketeers}, through the unchanged stage; this is a limited transfer check on two novels, not evidence of generalisation across genres or languages. The stage's language-model path, which calls a local model for characters with at least 25 mentions, is audited separately.

\subsection{Leakage Mitigations and Generic-Assistant Experiments}\label{sec:m-mitigation}

Every mitigation follows one rule: nothing visible at position $c$ may be computed from evidence after $c$. The mitigations differ only in where they enforce it.

\emph{Evidence-horizon versioning} (EHV) recomputes the deterministic profile for each attested chapter $c$ from the evidence and cumulative mention counts of chapters up to $c$, and writes only the keys whose value changed, stamped at $c$. Since the reader query already returns the latest-stamped value per key, the value served at $k$ is exactly what a build over chapters up to $k$ would hold. Body bindings carry a matching \texttt{confirmed\_at} stamp. \emph{The anchored pronoun cue} addresses a side effect of EHV, which withholds values whose pronouns settled early. It reads the first third-person pronoun after each mention, within the mention's own narration span, and is consulted only where the paragraph-level rule returns \emph{unknown}. Its setting was chosen by leave-one-serial-out selection and scored on the held-out serial. \emph{The prefix-decided gate} replaces the whole-volume mention count that decided whether to call the language model for a character with a count over the chapters read so far. We compare these repairs with the naive alternative, \emph{hiding every exposed fact}.

For generic assistants, we compare three designs with their reader-faithful counterparts on the confirmatory events, at a pre-registered cut just before the reveal chapter.

\emph{Whole-volume against versioned character cards.} A generator writes a card of at most 80 words about the aliased character from up to 12 passages spread over the alias's mentions. The whole-volume build W takes mentions from the entire book. The prefix build P takes only mentions before the cut and clips every passage at the cut. P is the truncate-and-rebuild reference and is identical to a card versioned by evidence horizon. Each build runs on original names and again under a frozen letter cipher. No model has seen the cipher names, so a leak in a renamed W card can only come from the input; a renamed P card should never leak, and this is our validity check. A card leaks if it tells the reader at the cut who the alias is, which we decide with two rules. The \emph{text rule} counts a leak when the card contains a name token of the identity that never occurs in the prefix. It is deterministic but applies only to the 19 events in which the identity's name first appears at the reveal. The \emph{judge rule} asks a fixed judge model, which is not one of the generators, whether the card states or clearly implies that the alias and the identity are the same person. It applies to all 54 events with alias mentions on both sides of the reveal. The two rules measure different things on different sets of events, and we never pool them. A robustness arm repeats W with an explicit instruction not to give away spoilers.

\emph{Suffix canaries and long context.} These tests alter only the suffix. Every identity name token after the cut becomes a freshly invented token, so the book still reveals the alias, but under a name no model has seen. We also plant one sentence, ``\emph{alias} had been born in \emph{invented place}'', after the first alias mention that follows the reveal. A hit is an exact string match of a planted token in the output, so it can be neither a false positive nor a product of memory. We apply the canaries to the W card (with the P card as comparison) and to a long-context assistant that is told where the reader is. That assistant receives up to 24,000 characters before the cut, the line \texttt{[READER POSITION: the reader has read up to here]}, and up to 16,000 characters after it, including the reveal, with instructions to answer only from the text before the marker and to reveal nothing after it. The question is ``Who is \emph{alias}?''. The comparison condition is \emph{physical truncation}: the same prompt with the suffix removed. The harness counts as valid only if the P-card and truncated conditions never produce a hit.

\emph{Alias-merged against prefix-built retrieval.} A canary cannot catch a leak that moves no suffix text, so the selection test uses events whose identity is already named somewhere in the reader's prefix. At index time, a resolver model reads the reveal window and decides whether the alias and the identity are the same person. Retrieval for the alias then returns up to 12 passages spread over the pre-cut mentions of both names, each clipped at the cut (an assertion in the code enforces this), and the card prompt runs on cipher names so that memory cannot help. The comparison is a \emph{prefix-causal index} built from the prefix alone, which has no reveal to merge on. The judge model labels leaks in this test, and this is the case for which it is least validated (Section~\ref{sec:r-assistant}).

\emph{Pseudonymise and map back} is the mitigation for memory: it replaces the names in the input with pseudonyms, runs the model, and maps names in the answer back to the originals. It was pre-registered with a non-inferiority margin of 5 points on accuracy after the reveal and on plain controls.

\emph{Statistical protocol.} Items in the same event share a book, a reveal and often a passage, so we resample events, not items, throughout. Intervals for gaps are 95\% percentile intervals from an event-cluster bootstrap \cite{efron1993bootstrap,dror2018hitchhiker}, using 5,000 resamples (seed 20261019) for the exploratory analyses and 10,000 (seed 20261101) for the confirmatory one. Item-pooled estimates are used only in the exploratory analyses and event-weighted estimates only in the confirmatory one; random-effects pooling is used only for the pooled confirmatory gap (H1). When the exploratory analysis pools several models, their items are combined within each event before events are resampled, which keeps the dependence between items from the same model and between models answering the same event. Exploratory results come with two-sided exact sign tests over events. The confirmatory hypotheses were one-sided at $\alpha=0.05$. The primary contrast P1 (high- and mid-fame events minus low-fame events) was tested alone at 0.05, as set out in the registration's final addendum, and so lies outside the Holm correction \cite{holm1979simple}, which covers the family \{H1, H2, H3, H5\} at the pre-registered family-wise level. Wherever a $p$-value appears, ``raw'' and ``Holm-adjusted'' say which it is. The pooled confirmatory gap (H1) combines the per-model event-weighted estimates in a DerSimonian--Laird random-effects model \cite{dersimonian1986meta} with a Hartung--Knapp interval \cite{hartung2001tests}. Random effects were pre-registered because the true gaps of different models may differ, but three models are too few to estimate the between-model variance. The DerSimonian--Laird estimate of $\tau^2$ was 0, so the pooled point estimate equals the precision-weighted average of the three per-model gaps, and the Hartung--Knapp interval uses a $t$ distribution with two degrees of freedom, which makes it deliberately wide. We therefore read H1 as a summary of these three models, not as an estimate of the mean gap over a population of open models. The primary evidence is the per-model estimates (Section~\ref{sec:r-memory}), all three of which include zero. Associations with familiarity use Spearman's $\rho$ with a permutation $p$-value. Proportions in the pipeline audit carry Wilson intervals \cite{wilson1927probable}, whose statistical unit is the character-cut. Cuts are nested within only three serials, so these intervals, and an event-cluster bootstrap that treats cuts as units, understate the uncertainty; with three serials no resampling at the serial level can support population inference. The pipeline intervals are therefore descriptive measures for the tested serials and systems, not estimates of how often leakage occurs across narrative systems. The confirmatory study's rules, prompts, events and thresholds were written down and hashed before the data they govern were collected \cite{nosek2018preregistration}, and any incomplete model run is kept out of pooled tests and reported on its own. An earlier version of the exploratory analysis treated items as independent; event resampling widened the intervals, and all results reported here use it.

\emph{Reproducibility.} The quoted values are recomputed from stored result files by scripts in the project repository (see Declarations), and a consistency script checks each value in a manifest against the manuscript. A reproducibility map in the supplementary material names, for each main table and figure, the stored output files and the script that computes it. Model outputs are stored, so the statistics regenerate without new model calls; regenerating the outputs themselves needs the same models and, for the audit, the copyrighted serials. Pre-registrations and event manifests are hashed. The Project Gutenberg texts are public domain and can be downloaded again using the identifiers listed in the supplementary material. The three web serials are copyrighted and are not redistributed, so the audit on them can only be reproduced by someone who holds the same texts. Timings are wall-clock measurements on the stated hardware; the local and rented GPU figures describe what these experiments cost and are not a controlled hardware benchmark.

\emph{Confirmatory study.} The pre-registered study tested the effect on 68 new events from 65 books with three open models. Each model answered 2,220 questions: the 453 items of all 68 events in four arms (A, B, B2 and B3, giving 1,812 answers) plus the closed-book and title-assisted probes for each event in three arms (204 answers each). The primary analysis uses only the pre-reveal items of the 32 primary test events. Table~\ref{tab:confirm} gives the per-model gaps and the pre-registered tests. No model showed a gap from original names: $+4.4$ points [$-2.3$, $+12.0$] for Mistral-Small-3.2-24B, $+0.0$ [$-4.4$, $+4.9$] for OLMo-2-32B and $-0.8$ [$-4.7$, $+2.3$] for Qwen2.5-32B. The random-effects pool was $+0.1$ [$-5.0$, $+5.2$] (H1, raw one-sided $p=0.47$). The primary familiarity contrast (P1), the gap for high- and mid-fame events minus the gap for low-fame events, pointed the expected way ($+7.6$ points [$-1.4$, $+18.3$], raw one-sided $p=0.059$), but the design could detect differences of only about 12.8 points with 80\% power, so the result is inconclusive. The correlation between fame and gap (H3, $\rho=+0.30$ over 32 events) passed its raw criterion and failed after Holm correction (adjusted $p=0.145$). In the closed-book probe, each model named the hidden identity in only 2 of 68 events. A post hoc check with a fixed rule, dropping the two primary events whose mined ``identity'' is a descriptive phrase rather than a name, gave $-1.1$ [$-3.8$, $+1.6$]. The unseen-story control (17 model-written stories) produced gaps of exactly zero for all three models. A recap task produced no spoiler in 144 outputs per condition (judged by another of the three models, so model-judged), and checking 62 books against a public index of the OLMo~2 pretraining corpus showed no link between a book's presence and its gap (Spearman $+0.13$, $p=0.41$).

The confirmatory null and the exploratory finding need not conflict. Most of the confirmatory sample comes from little-known books (25 of the 32 primary events), and the closed-book probe suggests the open models remember little about them, whereas the exploratory books include several of the best-known novels in English. That pattern is consistent with a familiarity-dependent effect (Section~\ref{sec:r-cue}). We offer this as a plausible interpretation, not as a demonstrated moderator or as the explanation of the failed replication: familiarity was never manipulated, the contrast meant to test it was underpowered and inconclusive, and the two studies also differ in their models and in how events were mined, so other explanations remain open. The incomplete fourth arm (211 of 1,218 planned answers) is excluded from every pooled estimate above and is not combined with the three-model confirmatory analysis.

\subsection{Cue-Preserving Controls and Book Familiarity}\label{sec:r-cue}

The letter cipher lowered accuracy on plain controls compared with original names (for Qwen2.5-7B on v2, from 95.4\% to 80.3\%), so part of an original-minus-cipher gap could in principle reflect lost reading cues rather than memory. Cue-preserving pseudonyms brought control accuracy back to the original level (96.7\% for the same model; all models are listed in the supplementary material) but did not remove the leakage gap (Figure~\ref{fig:cue} and Table~\ref{tab:cue}). Pooled over the 31 v2 and v3 events for the models that passed the validity gate, the gap was $+14.6$ [$+7.0$, $+22.7$] against the cipher and $+16.1$ [$+8.2$, $+25.0$] against pseudonyms, and the recovered fraction was $-0.11$ [$-0.36$, $+0.06$]. The pre-registered decision rule counts a recovered fraction below one third as memory-dominant, and the entire interval falls below that line, so restoring name cues brings back at most a small part of the gap. Because the interval includes zero and small negative values, the point estimate should be read as ``close to zero'', not as a sign that pseudonyms increase leakage. The result is consistent with memory tied to the particular names, but it does not establish the model's internal mechanism, and other effects of the name transformation remain possible.

\emph{Familiarity (post hoc).} After the v6 replication failed, we examined whether the gap varies with how well models recognise a book, measured as the number of the five local models that name the identity in the title-assisted probe (Figure~\ref{fig:dose}). For the valid local pool, the per-event gap rose with recognition (Spearman $+0.59$, permutation $p=0.00015$, 38 events). It was close to zero for books that at most three of the five models recognised ($+0.7$ [$-3.7$, $+5.5$] for the 20 events recognised by none or one, $-0.4$ [$-8.2$, $+6.2$] for the 6 recognised by two or three) and large for books recognised by four or five models ($+30.3$ [$+15.3$, $+43.8$], 12 events). The reference model's gap was positive in every group and larger for recognised books (Spearman $+0.37$, $p=0.024$). The analysis is post hoc, the groups are small (the six recognition levels hold 10, 10, 4, 2, 5 and 7 events), and for the local pool the predictor and the outcome come from the same models, so this is not an independent causal estimate. The pre-registered familiarity contrast (P1, Section~\ref{sec:r-memory}: $+7.6$ points [$-1.4$, $+18.3$] between the 7 high- and mid-fame and the 25 low-fame primary events) was inconclusive. We therefore treat the pattern as an exploratory association, not causal evidence, and it does not establish familiarity as a moderator. It is compatible with the v6 replication having failed because its books are little known, without showing that familiarity is the cause, and a nonsignificant or inconclusive result is not proof that an effect is absent.

\emph{Divergence is not the same as disclosure.} Of the 41 divergent character-cuts, annotator 2 judged 18 to be genuine premature disclosures (2.6\% of 685 cuts, Wilson [1.7, 4.1]\%) and 21 to be already supported by the text up to the cut. In the remaining 2 the direction was reversed: the status quo served \emph{unknown} where the prefix build held the correct value. With a permissive prompt the rate was 2.0\%, and on the original 289 cuts annotator 1 gave 1.7\% where annotator 2 gave 3.5\%. All three readings rule out zero, so we summarise the rate as a range, 1.7--3.5\% of character-cuts. The two annotators agreed with $\kappa=0.61$ on the 117 characters both labelled and never disagreed about male versus female. Both are models, not independent human annotators, and one of them implemented the repair, a potential source of bias that these rates inherit. We therefore treat the disclosure rates as provisional and the deterministic divergence and dependence tests as the more independently reproducible evidence.

\emph{A human check of the disclosure labels.} Table~\ref{tab:human_pipeline} summarises the 48-case check. The two annotators of a case agreed on gender in 38 cases (79.2\%, Wilson [65.7, 88.3]\%, Fleiss $\kappa=0.64$). In nine of the ten disagreements one annotator committed to a gender and the other declined to; only one was male against female. Seven of the ten fell in the stratum of divergent cuts that the models had called genuine disclosures, so that is where the people differ most. The guideline was not followed evenly. The first author reported labelling many cases from the character's name, which the guideline excludes, and their labels fit that report: they never answered ``undetermined'' (0 of 32 cases, against 7 and 8 of 32 for the other two annotators), they reported knowing the book's plot in 22 of 32 cases (0 and 5 for the others), and in all 7 of their disagreements with a partner they gave a gender where the partner declined. Wherever the first author was one of a split pair, we therefore let the other annotator's label stand. Where the two annotators agreed, their shared label stands; this reverses one earlier ruling in which the first author had overturned an agreed ``undetermined''. Where the two other annotators split, which happened for gender in 3 cases and for support in 3, an external reader settled the case. That reader is not an author, had read none of the books, and labelled the cases from the passages alone, treating a name as no evidence. This settled the 3 gender splits and 2 of the 3 support splits; the third support split was not among the cases that reader received and is left out of the support counts. We adopted this rule after seeing the label distributions, not before, so we report the original rule, in which the first author ruled on every split, beside it to keep the choice visible.

Under the revised rule, 9 of the 10 divergent cuts that the models had called genuine disclosures were judged not backed by the passages (90.0\%, Wilson [59.6, 98.2]\%); under the original rule it was 2 (20.0\%, Wilson [5.7, 51.0]\%). The raw pairs are less uniform: both annotators judged the served value not supported in 3 (30.0\%, Wilson [10.8, 60.3]\%) and at least one did in all 10, and in 5 of the 7 cases where only one did, the one who found it supported was the first author. The controls behaved as expected. Of the 8 non-divergent controls with a served value and a resolved label, none was judged unsupported (Wilson [0.0, 32.4]\%), and of the 8 cuts the models had judged supported, 6 were confirmed (75.0\%, Wilson [40.9, 92.9]\%; 7 under the original rule). Across the 34 cuts with a served value and a resolved label, 11 (32.4\%, Wilson [19.1, 49.2]\%) were not backed by the passages, against 4 of 35 (11.4\%, Wilson [4.5, 26.0]\%) under the original rule. Ten of the eleven were right according to the book, which is the difference between correctness and faithfulness: a value can match the novel and still be something the reader could not yet have known. Against the revised labels the model annotators agreed in 17 of 18 cases (annotator 1, $\kappa=0.91$), 37 of 48 (annotator 2, strict, $\kappa=0.65$) and 25 of 33 (annotator 2, permissive, $\kappa=0.61$), against 14 of 18, 31 of 48 and 25 of 33 under the original rule. The external reader, who knew none of the books, labelled all 11 cases that the first author had ruled on, and taking their labels for all 11 gives the same counts as the revised rule (9 of 10 and 11 of 34). The 4 repeated cases were labelled identically on gender in 8 of 8 annotator-repeats and on support in 7 of 8. \emph{Truncation is a sound but incomplete test.} The counterfactual suffix found 47 of the 289 original character-cuts (16.3\%) whose served gender changed when only later chapters changed, against the 23 that truncate-and-rebuild detected. All 23 divergent cuts were among the 47, and none of the 47 lay outside an exposure window. The counterfactual rebuild compared 304 character-cuts, of which the 289 truncation cuts give the 47 above (51 of 304 in all); under versioning, none of the 304 changed.

\emph{The model path.} With the same prompt in both builds, the local model's discrete trait differed in 0 of 101 character-cut pairs, so at these settings later chapters did not alter how the model read a character. The gate that decides whether to call the model did depend on the future: it used a whole-volume mention count, so 50 of 296 pairs received the model's reading in the full build and the deterministic one in the prefix build. Deciding the gate chapter by chapter removed all 50.

\subsection{Character Cards, Long Context, and Selection Leakage}\label{sec:r-assistant}

\emph{Whole-volume against versioned cards (content leakage).} Table~\ref{tab:cards} and Figure~\ref{fig:cards} compare the two builds. On cipher names, which no model has ever seen, the whole-volume card named the hidden identity before the reveal in 18.5\% of 54 events under the judge rule and in 24.6\% of 19 events under the text rule; the versioned card did so in 1.2\% and 0.0\%. The two rules are different metrics on different event sets. The pre-registered input-channel contrast, W minus P on renamed text, was $+17.3$ points [$+9.3$, $+26.5$] under the judge rule and $+24.6$ [$+8.8$, $+42.1$] under the text rule. The memory-only contrast, the versioned card on original names minus the same card on cipher names, was $+2.5$ points under the judge rule, so for these books and models the build was a larger source of leakage than the model's memory. Larger models (24--32B) showed the same pattern (whole-volume 23.1\% against versioned 1.9\%, judge rule). Telling the model to avoid spoilers did not stop the leak: the instructed whole-volume card still leaked in 14.8\% of events under the judge rule (difference from the versioned card $+14.8$ [$+5.6$, $+24.1$]) and in 15.8\% under the text rule, where the interval of the difference included zero ([$0.0$, $+31.6$]). Checked against the deterministic text rule on 380 card labels, the judge caught 91.7\% of text-rule leaks and flagged 0.9\% of non-leaks ($\kappa=0.92$), and a second judge from another model family (OLMo-2-32B) agreed with it on 97.8\% of 684 labels ($\kappa=0.88$). The judge's accuracy on the 35 events outside the text rule's scope has not been established on real cards, so the judge-rule result over all 54 events is model-judged and not fully model-independent. To bound the judge where the answer is known without any annotator, we ran it, with the same prompt, model and decoding, on 120 short synthetic cards with invented names, half of which state or imply that the alias and the identity are one person (Supplement, section ``Known-truth test of the card judge''). It found all 30 explicit identifications (100.0\%, Wilson [88.6, 100.0]\%) and 19 of 30 implied ones (63.3\%, Wilson [45.5, 78.1]\%), and called 59 of 60 negatives correct (98.3\%, Wilson [91.1, 99.7]\%), including 29 of 30 hard negatives in which both names appear but belong to different people. A judge from another model family (Gemma-2-9B) agreed with the truth on 54 of 60 positives and all 60 negatives, and the two judges agreed on 112 of 120 cards. The judge is therefore conservative: it misses part of what a card only implies and rarely invents an identification, so the judge-rule leak rates are more likely understated than overstated. The test uses templated cards and our own definition of an implied identification, so it supports this direction without replacing validation on real cards. The versioned-card rates hold for the tested prompts, generators and configurations and are not proof that prefix-built cards are spoiler-free. Prefix-built cards on cipher names, for instance, leaked in 0.0\% of events under the text rule but in 1.2\% under the judge rule. Their input contains no suffix text, so a judge-rule positive there is either a judge error or an identification the prefix itself supports, not suffix leakage; we report it as observed rather than rounding it to zero.

\emph{Suffix canaries (content leakage, deterministic).} Table~\ref{tab:canary} and Figure~\ref{fig:leaks} report the canary experiments, which need no judge. The whole-volume card repeated a token planted after the cut in 22.8\% of 54 events, against 0\% for the versioned card. The long-context assistant that had been told where the reader is (up to 24,000 characters before the cut, a reader-position marker line, and up to 16,000 characters after it including the reveal) quoted a post-marker token in 40.6\% of 46 events by exact string match, against 0\% when the suffix was physically removed. All three generators leaked in both designs, and the versioned and truncated conditions produced no hits. These zero-hit controls validate the tested controls and the experimental harness; they do not show that every implementation of those designs is leak-free. The leak also went beyond the identity: the planted birthplace sentence appeared in 21.0\% of whole-volume cards (54 events) and 11.1\% of long-context answers (39 events; rates pooled over the three generators).

\emph{Alias-merged retrieval (selection leakage, judge-dependent).} In 24 of the 36 events whose identity is named in the prefix, the resolver merged the alias with the identity after reading the reveal window, and retrieval then served only passages the reader had already read. Cards built from those passages still identified the alias with the identity in 19.4\% of merged events, compared with 1.4\% for the prefix-causal index (paired event-level difference $+18.1$ points [$+4.2$, $+33.3$]), and all three generators leaked. A canary cannot catch this leak, because no suffix token ever enters the input, and a timestamp filter on served passages lets it through. Leaving out the four merged events whose mined identity is a descriptive phrase (post hoc, fixed rule) gave $+18.3$ [$+3.3$, $+36.7$] over 20 events. Both rates, 19.4\% and 1.4\%, are model-judged; no person labelled these cards, so the selection result is not independently human-validated. The judge is least validated in this setting, where merged retrieval puts both names into the card, so we tested it against known truth on a second set of 120 synthetic cards, distinct from the card-judge test above and built for this purpose (supplementary material). These were card-length texts in the generators' format, on cipher-like invented names; half named both people as clearly different characters who meet or are described side by side, and half stated or implied that they are one person. The judge called none of the 60 two-name negatives an identification (0 of 60, Wilson upper bound 6.0\%), found all 30 explicit identifications and missed 16 of 30 implied ones; a second judge from another family (Gemma-2-9B) agreed with it on 111 of 120 cards. On this evidence, judge false positives on cards that merely name both people are too rare to produce a 19.4\% rate, and the misses on implied identifications make the merged rate more likely understated than overstated. The test uses templated cards, so it bounds the judge's error modes without replacing human labels on the real cards, and we still read the selection result as preliminary evidence for the mechanism.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\emph{Why our strongest claim is about pipelines, not memory.} Within the implementations we tested, the pipeline results hold across generators and leak rules, and the deterministic instruments (truncate-and-rebuild, counterfactual suffixes and canaries) show them without any judge. They also follow from how the stages are built: a stage that reads the whole volume and stamps its output at an earlier position can diverge from a prefix build whenever its evidence arrives later than its stamp, whichever model it uses. In the card experiment on the same events, the input channel was far larger than the memory-only channel. For assistants working over books like these, what the pipeline feeds the model is a larger risk than what the model remembers, and an easier one to control.

\emph{Why name-keyed memory looks conditional, and why the null result matters.} The exploratory and confirmatory studies differ in the books, the models and the way events were mined, so together they point to a possible familiarity-conditional pattern that the pre-registered analysis did not confirm. The confirmatory null is worth reporting because it bounds the effect on less familiar material, shows with the unseen-story control that the harness does not invent gaps, and keeps the exploratory result from being read as a general property of language models. The familiarity moderator remains unproven, since the evidence for it is post hoc and the pre-registered test lacked power.

\emph{Engineering implications.} Filtering passages or marking a reader position is not enough if upstream derived state or selection decisions have already used future information. The marker failed because the suffix was still in the model's input, and the filter failed because the suffix had already been used to decide what to filter. We expect both points to carry over to other systems as design arguments, although the rates need not. For design, this means that whether a system is reader-faithful depends on where in the pipeline the rule is enforced, and each place where it is enforced trades faithfulness against utility. The repairs that reduced leakage all apply one rule, that anything visible at $c$ is computed from $P(c)$ alone, at four different places: derived facts (evidence-horizon versioning), summaries (prefix-built cards), model input (physical truncation) and indexing (prefix-causal indexes). The counterfactual-suffix result also bears on testing. Truncate-and-rebuild on a single volume misses any dependence that happens to agree with the book, so a system meant to be faithful should be tested with altered suffixes as well as removed ones.

\emph{Costs and limits of the mitigations.} Reader-faithfulness and correctness are different goals. Versioning withheld values that the prefix supported, the anchored cue that recovered them did not transfer to another corpus, and pseudonymisation reduced disclosure only by reducing accuracy. The repairs worked in our tests, but that is not a formal guarantee. Definition~\ref{def:rf} ranges over every possible suffix, whereas our tests are sampled operational checks and not a verification method: they cover a removed suffix and one pronoun swap, on one codebase and a small set of models. A guarantee for arbitrary books, pipelines and models would need faithfulness to be built in by construction and verified against the code.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}\label{sec:limits}

\emph{Construct validity.} An explicit reveal sentence marks when the book states an identity, not when a reader could first infer it, so premature naming against this oracle can include reasonable inference from clues. Ambiguity of this kind plausibly affects every arm equally, but even then it could enlarge or shrink the estimated gap between arms, and it blurs what ``premature'' means for a single item. In the human check nobody judged an identity confidently inferable before the reveal, yet at least one annotator suspected it in 17 of 36 events. In many events, then, the reveal sentence confirms what earlier clues have already hinted at, and ``premature'' should be read as ``before the book states it''. The memory results hold only under that definition of prematurity.

\emph{Event-selection bias.} Language models proposed and audited every event. Two of them, the confirmatory miner and auditor, were also among the models evaluated, and selection was restricted to reveals stated in sentences that match fixed cue patterns. Some mined events are of poor quality: seven of the 68 confirmatory events have a descriptive phrase as their ``identity'', and in the human check one annotator judged 6 of the 18 validated confirmatory events invalid, although the other annotator accepted them. The events are therefore not a representative sample of concealed identities in fiction, and the effects could differ for implicit, gradual or visual reveals.

\emph{External validity.} The corpora are public-domain Western fiction, three translated web serials and one synthetic volume. The models are five small local models, three open 24--32B models and one commercial reference model. The results do not establish rates for contemporary serial fiction, other languages, other genres or other assistant architectures.

\emph{Reveal-check validity (36 events).} This check asks whether the reveal sentence states the identity explicitly. Two author-annotators labelled each event; they did not see model labels but knew the study's hypotheses. One of them judged six of the 18 confirmatory events invalid while the other accepted them, so the check bounds event quality without settling it. It does not validate the whole event corpus or show that the identity was unknowable from earlier passages: annotators suspected it in many events, so ``premature'' in the memory results means before the book states it.

\emph{Disclosure-label validity (48 cuts).} This separate check asks whether a divergent pipeline value is a genuine disclosure. Apart from the two human checks, every non-deterministic label comes from a model: the disclosure labels (two model annotators, one of which implemented the repair, so evaluator bias is possible), the card judge rule and the selection-leak judge. The judge was validated against a deterministic rule only where that rule applies and against known truth only on synthetic cards, so the judge-rule and selection-leak results are model-judged and not independently human-validated. The 48-cut sample is purposive, and its three author-annotators knew the hypotheses. The first author's labels were partly name-based, against the guideline, and were set aside wherever they conflicted with another annotator's. That rule was adopted after the label distributions were seen, and it moves the count of sampled model-judged disclosures that the passages do not back from 2 to 9 of 10, so we report both rules. One support split still has no resolved label and is left out. The annotators saw at most eight passages while the model annotators read up to 24, so ``not supported'' means not backed by what the annotators saw. The check bounds the model labels without giving a rate, and the disclosure rates remain provisional.

\emph{Statistical power and inference.} The confirmatory primary set is small and has few mid- or high-fame books, so a null result there cannot exclude a modest effect and the familiarity contrast is inconclusive; the selection test rests on a small number of merged events. The pipeline audit consists of nested cuts from three serials, which do not support population-level estimates. Its intervals describe uncertainty for the tested data and are not estimates of leakage prevalence across narrative systems.

\emph{Implementation dependence.} The pipeline audit concerns one narrative graph's persona stage, one card prompt, one long-context layout and one resolver design, and the rates apply to those implementations. The mechanisms and the tests should carry over to other systems, but the numbers need not.

\emph{Developer as auditor.} The authors built the narrative graph, the repairs and the assistant designs that they audit, so the audit may have been tuned towards flattering results. Three features limit this risk. First, the headline pipeline results come from deterministic instruments (truncate-and-rebuild, counterfactual suffixes, canaries) that involve no judgement by the authors, and the audit reports failures of the authors' own system first and in numbers (82 of 289 character-cuts diverge under the naive repair, and the card and long-context designs leak). Second, each repair is compared with the naive alternative it replaces, and the same tests are re-run on the repaired system. Third, the pre-registered memory study was fixed before the confirmatory data, and its null result is reported in full. An independent re-audit by another group is still desirable, and the repository is released so that one can be run.

\emph{Scope of the limitations.} The limitations above attach to specific claims and not to the paper as a whole. The existence of pipeline dependence in the audited persona stage rests on deterministic comparison alone, so it does not depend on model labels, on the human checks or on event selection; by Definition~\ref{def:rf} one divergence is enough to show a violation. The zero results, namely zero observed divergence under versioning, zero text-rule leaks and zero canary hits for prefix-built cards, and zero canary hits under physical truncation, are claims about the tested configurations and harness, and are worded that way. The model-judged rates for prefix-built cards (1.2\%) and the prefix-causal index (1.4\%) are above zero and are reported as such. The model-judged and exploratory results carry the evidence labels set in Section~\ref{sec:setup} and are not used to support the deterministic claims. Where a limitation does weaken a claim, namely the memory result, the disclosure rate and the selection-leak rate, the text says so at the point of the claim.

\emph{Reproducibility and access.} The public-domain texts, prompts and analysis code are available. The reported statistics can be regenerated from the stored outputs, but exact model outputs may not be: the commercial model may change behind its API, checkpoint digests were recovered after the runs rather than recorded at run time, and the serial texts cannot be redistributed.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}

We defined reader-faithfulness as non-interference from the unread part of a book, separated the fixed configuration from book-derived state, and tested the property with sampled operational checks of parametric memory and of pipeline and assistant designs.

For RQ1, a name-keyed memory effect appeared for the reference model in the exploratory setting but was not confirmed in the pre-registered study, so it remains a possible familiarity-conditional effect to be tested directly. For RQ2, processing that depends on future text produced measurable dependence and leakage in every design we tested. In a component-level audit, evidence-horizon versioning removed the observed divergence of the persona stage at a cost in correctness that we report, physical truncation produced no suffix-canary hits, and prefix-built cards produced no deterministic leaks, though a model judge still flagged a small share of them. For RQ3, entity-merge decisions made with later text disclosed hidden identities even though every served passage came from the prefix. That result is model-judged and not independently human-validated; a known-truth test bounds the judge's false-positive rate but does not replace human labels.

For engineering practice, position markers and timestamp filters are insufficient when upstream processing has already used future information. The paper does not establish spoiler-free behaviour for arbitrary books, models or pipelines.

We see three directions for future work. The first is a larger independent human-annotated sample that records both how explicit a reveal is and whether the identity is already inferable from the prefix, with disagreements settled by a person who has not read the books, together with human-validated disclosure labels on text that can be shared or on synthetic text with known truth. The second is a prospective test that varies familiarity using matched famous and obscure books. The third is to enforce evidence horizons by construction in a pipeline, so that faithfulness can be checked against the code instead of only measured.
